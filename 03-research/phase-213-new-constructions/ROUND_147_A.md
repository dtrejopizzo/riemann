# A complete detector with the same limiting density as the already signed average

Let p(y)=sinc(3y)^4 normalized to mass one, T=log x, and U as in 144. For V>0 put K_V(y)=p(y)exp(−y²/(2V)), unnormalized, and J_V(T)=∫K_V(y)U(T+y)dy. Take V=j log(j+2), j=floor(log₂ T). The global sign is equivalent to J_V(T)≤0 for all sufficiently large T. Under RH J_V(T)→a=1/ζ(1/2)<0; outside RH there are positive values in each large [2^j,2^(j+1)]. Truncating to |y|≤3√(TV) preserves both statements and gives finite data. After normalizing, the weight has variance ≤1/12 and converges to p, just like the unconditional one of 146.

**Under RH.** Write M_V(s)=∫K_V(y)e^(sy)dy. The spectral estimate of 146 holds with V in place of cT and √V in the prefactor. On the line σ=1/T, for |τ|≥13, (|τ|−12)² dominates a positive absolute multiple of 1+log(2+|τ|). The Gaussian decay beats the bound exp(C log T log(2+|τ|)) for the reciprocal of ζ in 144, since V/log T→∞. In |τ|≤13 there are no nontrivial zeros. Shifting the segment to −1/4 leaves o(1) on the horizontals and the left edge. The residue is a M_V(0)→a. With |U(T+y)|≤exp((T+y)/2) the symmetric physical truncation costs o(1) uniformly in the band of 144.

**The factor that survives at an off-line pole.** In angular frequency, the Fourier transform of p is a nonnegative cubic spline supported in [−12,12]. Near −12 it is C(ξ+12)³, C>0, by convolution of the four indicators. For any fixed pole z=b+iγ with γ>12, the Fourier formula of 146 gives

    M_V(z) ~ C' V^(−7/2) exp(V(z−12i)²/2)/(γ−12−ib)^4,    C'>0.

Indeed, put ξ=−12+u. After extracting the exponential at the endpoint, exp(−V(γ−12−ib)u−Vu²/2) remains. Outside a fixed neighbourhood of zero the relative error against the exponential factor is exponentially small. Inside, change u to w/V and use ∫₀∞ w³ exp(−(γ−12−ib)w)dw=6/(γ−12−ib)^4. The same argument after differentiating a fixed number k of times gives M_V^(k)(z)/M_V(z)=O_z,k(V^k). For γ<−12 use conjugation. Thus a pole of large height keeps a nonzero coefficient of size exp(−O_z(V)), even though p has compactly supported Fourier transform.

**Converse.** If there is an off-line zero, repeat the wide detection of 144 at N=2^j, keeping V constant throughout the convolution: average J_V with a Gaussian of variance cN at centres vN, 1.4≤v≤1.6, with c fixed small depending on a pole with b>0. The contour integrand is now R(s) M_V(s) exp(N(vs+cs²/2)). M_V costs exp(o(N)) on the sides of the contour and on the right line, uniformly in height there by its Gaussian bound. Choose a subinterval with a single dominant pair of positive rate and uniform gap as in 143–144. For a pole of multiplicity m the complex leading term of the residue contains M_V(z)N^(m−1)(v+cz)^(m−1) times a nonzero constant. The lower terms have relative error in the envelope O_z(V/N+1/N) thanks to the derivative bound. The phase of M_V(z) is fixed within the band of centres; the phase of the leading term runs through ≍N turns in the subinterval. Hence there is a positive wide average ≥exp(dN) for some d>0, since V=o(N). If the short datum were nonpositive throughout the band, this would be impossible: outside [N,2N] one has |J_V(t)|≪√V exp(t/2+V/8), and in the wide convolution that part costs O(exp(−N)) by choosing c small enough. The same contradiction is obtained with the physical truncation by the uniform o(1) error.

**Which contribution remains to be controlled.** Let W=c₀T, c₀=1.25·10^(−25), as in 146. For sufficiently large T, W>V and K_W−K_V≥0. Its mass is

    ∫(K_W−K_V)dy ~ 1/(24V).

Indeed V(1−exp(−y²/(2V)))≤y²/2 and ∫y²p=1/12; by dominated convergence the mass deficit of K_V is asymptotic to 1/(24V), and that of K_W is O(1/W). The integral of U against K_W is a+o(1) unconditionally. To sign the short datum it would then suffice, for example, to have a uniform lower bound strictly larger than a for the integral of U against K_W−K_V in the lower limit. Its small mass alone does not give such a bound: the cancellations of L in that integral remain to be controlled. This isolates a single positive-weight correction between the proved average and the detector.

**Antecedents.** 144 uses the semigroup with Gaussians; 146 obtains the sign after the sinc⁴ factor at linear auxiliary variance. The new step is to prove that the same factor still detects poles when the auxiliary regularization changes, through the nonzero coefficient at the edge of its transform. Variance and limiting density alone do not decide the reach of the test.
