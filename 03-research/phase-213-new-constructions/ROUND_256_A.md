# A budget of 1/3 for a finite sum of positive prime contributions

Write for integer N≥1 and prime p

    w_N(p)=N/p² ∫₀¹ z(1−z)²(1−z/p)^(N−1)dz > 0,
    A_N(P)=Σ_(p≤P) w_N(p)log p,    A_N=A_N(∞).

The global sign is equivalent to A_N(N^(3/2))≤1/3 for every sufficiently large N. Under RH the inequality is strict for EVERY N and every cut P. If it fails, for each 1/2<d<Θ there are infinitely many N with A_N(N^(3/2))>1/3+c N^(d−1), for some constant c>0. Each w is rational and all summands are positive; an excess on accumulating primes up to any finite cut is already a witness against RH. The absence of excesses in a finite search does not prove the universal inequality.

**Origin of the budget.** Set A₀=0. Exactly A_N=Σ_(n<N)b_n, and A_N→1/3. For the first identity sum the integrals of 255: the integrand is C(x)x^(−2)[1−(1−1/x)^N]. Here the bracketed factor is O(N/x) and allows interchange with the prime sum in absolute value. At each prime set z=p/x. The integral of k(z) is zero and its primitive is −z(1−z)²; one integration by parts gives w_N(p). For the second identity the classical PNT bound for θ(x)−x and the zero integral of k give C(x)=O(x exp(−c√log x)) by parts (separate p≤√x). Thus |C(x)|x^(−2) is integrable and the limit is F(1)=1/3, by the residue one of P(s) and K'(1)=1/3. In particular T_N=1/3−A_N=∫ C(x)x^(−2)(1−1/x)^N dx. Under RH, 247 and 248 give C≥0 at every x and C(x)≥0.17√x for sufficiently large x. Integrating over [2N,4N] gives T_N≫1/√N.

**Analysis of the converse.** T_N is bounded and b_n=T_n−T_(n+1). In 1<Re s<2, the continuation of 255 and summation by parts give that Γ(2−s)F(s)−(1−s)Σ_(n≥1)T_n n^(−s) extends holomorphically to 0<Re s<2. Indeed n^(1−s)−(n−1)^(1−s)=(1−s)n^(−s)+O_s(n^(−1−Re s)) for n≥2, uniformly on compacts. There is no real pole of the series of T_n at 1: on splitting the integral of T_n into x≤√n and x>√n, one obtains |T_n|≪exp(−c'√log n) by the PNT above. Thus the series converges absolutely at 1 and its continuation across 1 has a removable singularity. On the whole rest of the real strip (1/2,1) it is regular by 247. Every zero with real part >1/2 still gives a nonreal pole. If T_n≥−n^(d−1) eventually, the series of T_n+n^(d−1) has eventually nonnegative coefficients, is regular on the real axis above d and has a pole with real part >d. This contradicts Landau. Hence infinitely many excesses A_N>1/3+N^(d−1) remain.

The cost of truncating is ≤(N/12)Σ_(p>P)(log p)/p²≤N/(3P), by θ(y)≤2y and partial summation. At P=N^(3/2) that loss is o(N^(d−1)). The same argument holds with any P=N^(3/2+o(1)). To search for a violation only genuine primes and a sum without cancellation are needed.

**Certification of the initial budget.** A_N increases up to N=2^G by 255; moreover T_(2^G)>0, so A_N(P)<1/3 for ALL cuts P and N≤2^G. To check the endpoint, set N=2^G, integrate the formula of 248 against x^(−2−η)(1−1/x)^N and let η↓0. Normalise by B(1/2,N+1). The factor of x^z in the limit is ∏_(j=0)^N(j+1/2)/(j+1−z). The low zeros cost ≤0.094. For zeros of height >G the first factor has modulus ≤1/(2G); for j≥1 it is dominated by (j+1/2)/j and one moreover gains 2^(−G/2) from the first G indices. The remaining product costs ≤2√N; the total is ≤1/G. These same bounds dominate for η>0, justifying the limit in the absolutely weighted sum. The main term is kept as 4/15. The low zeros of J₂ cost ≤0.235 N^(−1/4) and the high ones ≤5·6.4·10^(−12). For 0≤z<1/2 the real ratio is ≤(N+1)^(−(1/2−z)) by the harmonic sum; a factor log x costs additionally ≤2+log(N+1) for z≤1/3. This makes the errors of 248 (constant and odd powers) cost together less than 0.001. The normalised margin is >0.17. It is not presupposed that the tail of the later b_n is positive term by term.

**What remains.** The pole mechanism and the use of squares come from 247–255. The practical difference is that a FINITE sum of positive numbers is compared with a universal rational budget, without knowing the other primes or approximating alternating differences of order N. The uniform estimate preventing more than 1/3 from being spent is open.
