                                                   1. A lightning introduction to roughly assess the results
                                              Since their discovery by V. Brun in [3, 4], sieve methods have proved very efficient
                                           to bound above quantities defined by the exclusion of some or several residue classes.
                                           Sieve methods are now classified in three main categories: the combinatorial sieves,
                                           the Selberg (or Λ2 ) sieve and the large sieves. Gallagher’s larger sieve introduced
                                           in [6] remains an isolated tool, though a powerful one where it applies. We are
                                           concerned in this paper with the large sieves. We only mention here that these
                                           arose from the initial work of Yu. V. Linnik in [13]; we refer the readers to the
                                           monographs [16] by H. L. Montgomery and [24] for more historical details.

On the method. We rely on (a slightly more general but essentially) the same
local lower estimate, but modify the global inequality. A first path goes through
an approximated Bessel inequality, like Proposition 1 in [1] by E. Bombieri. Mont-
gomery & Vaughan in [17] use a (weighted or not ) version of Hilbert’s inequality.
We refer the readers to [32] by W. Yangjit and to [5] by E. Carneiro & F. Littmann
for recent work on this inequality. Instead of that, we rely on the Farey dissection
of the unit interval, as in the circle method; our global inequality is a consequence
of the Parseval Identity. It is recalled in Lemma 4.1.

                                12. Additional notes
    The optimality of the present proof may be questioned, at three stations: the
local lower bound provided by Theorem 3.1, the lower bound in Lemma 4.1 and
the replacement of the characteristic function 1r´δq ,δq s pα ´ a{qq of the arc ra{q ´
δq , a{q ` δq s by the function Fq pα ´ a{qq “ D̂r0,N s,δq pα ´ aq q which has a sharp peak
at α “ a{q. The circle method philosophy relies (in most cases) on the fact that
the trigonometric polynomial Spαq has its maximum at rational points, and this
for varying values of N . Such a regularity should lead to a sharp peak at α “ a{q.
This argument goes in favour of saying that the replacement of 1r´δq ,δq s pα ´ a{qq
by Fq pα´a{qq does not loose much. And then we may believe that the lower bound
of Lemma 4.1 does not loose much either.
    As noted earlier, Theorem 3.1 may be thought as optimal in its form: when
we use the Cauchy’s inequality on the equality (15), the result is an equality
when Spa{qq and ψ̂q˚ paq are proportional and therefore a plausible set of values
for Spa{qq is available that takes care of the support condition. This being said,
Figures 1 and 2 seem to say that the lower bound in ?            Theorem 3.1 is often not
sharp enough when the modulus q becomes of the size N . It also hints that, if
we are to rely on a single modulus, then Theorem 3.1 will be hard to improve, as
the minimum seems to increase, but very slowly.
    The behaviour for a single q but varying length N is also relevant; the plots we
obtain show a very stable situation.
