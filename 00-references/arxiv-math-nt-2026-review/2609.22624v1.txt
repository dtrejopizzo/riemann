                                              The aim of this article is to provide bounds on the number of zeros in small
                                           sections of the critical strip, particularly along the boundary of the critical strip.
                                           We will look at primarily two types of regions, circular, and rectangular.
                                              Throughout the article we shall denote by
                                                  • N (T ) the number of zeros, ρ of ζ with imaginary 0 < ℑ(ρ) ≤ T .
                                                  • N (s, α) the number of zeros ρ of ζ with |ρ − s| ≤ α.
                                                  • N (T1 , T2 , α) the number of zeros ρ of ζ with T1 ≤ ℑ(ρ) ≤ T2 and ℜ(ρ) >
                                                    1 − α.
                                           In all cases zeros are counted with multiplicity and we shall maintain the convention
                                           that zeros on the boundary count with 1/2 their multiplicity. We note that using
                                           this last convention, as opposed to including or excluding the boundary does not
                                           impact the bounds we would prove except for the degenerate cases T = 0, α = 0,
                                           or T1 = T2 .
                                              Note that other sources write N (σ, T ) for the number of zeros ρ of ζ with
                                           0 < ℑ(ρ) ≤ T and ℜ(ρ) > σ. We don’t anticipate this causing any confusion.
                                              There are many results on zero density, however most of these consider T2 −T1 to
                                           be large in comparison to the size of T2 . Our aim is to treat much smaller intervals.
                                           At the same time, we note that bounds on the number of zeros in short intervals are
                                           a key ingredient to a common strategy for proving bounds on zeros in long intervals
                                           (see for example [5]).

   The above says that a positive proportion of zeros are not near the edge of the
critical strip in relatively small intervals. Tables 8 and 9 allow one to get many
more explicit examples of positive proportions, whereas Tables 10 and 11 illustrate
minimal values for t0 for which a positive proportion result is attainable.
   This result is in some ways qualitatively weaker than other estimates about
zeros being on or near the critical line. For example the approach of Levinson, as
improved by Conrey and then Pratt, Robles, Zaharescu and Zeindler (See [14, 26,
32]) establishes that, as t → ∞, a little over 40% are on the critical line. However,
the approach only appears to work on long intervals, that is, intervals of length at
least ω(T ). In fact, since roughly 40% of the zeros less than (5/4)T are less than
(3/4)T the result barely tells you that in an interval of width T /2 at height T there
must be any zeros on the critical line. The recent claimed improvement to 2/3 in
[1] doesn’t fundamentally alter this. Karatsuba’s proof of Selberg’s conjecture does
lower that threshold to powers of T , and subsequent work ultimately establishes
that almost every relatively short (sub-exponential in log(T )) interval contains zeros
on the critical line (see [24]). But almost all intervals still isn’t all, and they still
aren’t obtaining a positive proportion. Standard zero-density results going back at
least to Bohr and Landau (see [8]) tell us 100% of zeros are within ϵ of the critical
line. But again this tells us nothing about short intervals. In the opposite direction,
results of Montgomery in fact tell us that if there are zeros very near the edge of
the critical strip, there will somehow be many of them (see [27, Chapter 11]).

   These allow us to conclude that if B1,α,r < 1/4 or A1,α,r < 1/4 then there exists
a finite h0 , so that for t sufficiently large a positive proportion of zeros in intervals
of width at least h0 will have real part in [α, 1 − α].
   One approach we use relies on bounds for N (s, α). As such we provide updated
explicit versions of such bounds in Theorems 16 and 18. We note that such bounds
on N (s, α) are used in many applications directly. For example in log-free zero-
density results (see for example [5, Corollary 2.3] and [29, Lemmas 1 and 2]).
Various approaches to zero-free regions use these types of results as well (see for
example [4, 17, 27]). Bounds like those in Theorems 2 and 3 also have the potential
to be used to improve other zero density results such as in [3, 5, 30] where there can
be benefit in bounding the number of zeros in a rectangular region rather than a
square one.
   We organize this paper as follows:
      • In Section 1 we apply Jensen’s theorem to bound N (s, α). In order to
        provide sharp bounds we provide methods to improve estimates for the
        integral inside the critical strip in Section 1.1 and outside the critical strip
        in Section 1.2.
      • In Sections 2 and 3 we provide bounds on N (t − h, t + h, α) arising from
        two different zero detectors, the first of which is better for α smaller, and
        the second better for α larger.
      • In order to implement the approach of Section 3 in Section 3.1 we provide an
        improved mechanism to bound the arg integrals which arising as secondary
        error terms in more standard zero density results.
      • In Appendix A we provide a number of explicit bounds for log |ζ(s)| and
        log(Γ(s)) which we needed elsewhere.
   There are a number of avenues of future exploration which could improve the
result here, particularly for small t. Firstly, there are many choices for zero detectors
we could use for N (s, α). In this article we currently use a classical version of
Jensen’s formula. It would be interesting to explore other detectors such as those
                                                                            ′
in [5, 17, 18, 20, 35]. As these methods tend to involve estimating | ζζ |, or higher
                                      ′
derivatives, the ability to bound | ζζ | seems to be a limiting factor here. However,
following the approach of Section 2 it is possible that even if they can’t improve
results for N (1 + it, α), they could nonetheless provide improvements for bounds
on N (t − h, t + h, α).
   A second natural direction of exploration is parts of our analysis away from the
1-line to work at either 1 + η or 1 + η(t). This would almost certainly improve the
qualitative values obtained for smaller t by minimizing the coefficients of log log |t|
and the O(1) terms at the expense of the coefficient of log |t|.
   In the other direction, for extremely large values of t, and small values of α,
producing a variant of Theorem 24 using Theorem 18 would asymptotically be
better.
   If one wants an improved result for small values of h, then further developing Re-
mark 21 is appropriate, as would be developing alternatives to, or simply not using,
the optimizations of Section 2.1 since for short intervals bounding the integrand
will provide a better bound than bounding its anti-derivative at the endpoints.
