Structure of the paper. In order to prove Theorem 1.1, we rely on the following
guiding principle: a multiplicative function f : N → T is either close to some nit ,
for t ∈ R (we say that f is pretentious), in which case it behaves like the structured
function nit , or it is close to none of the nit (we say that f is non-pretentious), in
which case it behaves like a random function. In the pretentious case, the position
of f (n) on T can be controlled through the size of n. In the non-pretentious case, we
exploit the arithmetic structure of the integers to rotate f (n) towards a prescribed
direction (this is called the rotation trick, and was introduced by Klurman, Man-
gerel, Pohoata and Teräväinen in [10]). This P    philosophy is motivated by P   Halász’s
theorem, which asserts that the mean value x1 n≤x f (n) of f is close to x1 n≤x nit
if f is close to some nit , and tends to 0 otherwise. This theorem is one of the cor-
nerstones of pretentious multiplicative number theory, developed by Granville and
Soundararajan in [6]. We make an extensive use of tools from pretentious theory,
which we collect in Section 2.
   In Section 3, we establish a few technical lemmas on the distribution of multi-
plicative functions on the unit circle.
   In Sections 4 and 5, we prove Theorem 1.1. In Section 4, we first reduce to the
case where f K1 and g K2 are both pretentious for some integers K1 , K2 ≥ 1. We then
complete the proof in the pretentious setting in Section 5. To this end, we assume,
without loss of generality, that f K1 is close to nit but is not equal to it. In particular,
there exists a prime p such that f (p) ̸= pit , and its powers provide an independent
source of density, ultimately leading to a contradiction.
   In Section 6, we prove Proposition 1.5, establishing the limits of possible general-
izations of Theorem A.
   Finally, in Section 7, we prove Corollaries 1.6 and 1.7.

   Finally, we state a concentration inequality for pretentious functions. Roughly
speaking, it asserts that whenever f pretends to χnit , the values of f (n) are, on
average, well approximated by χnit , provided that n is restricted to a suitable arith-
metic progression. The need for such a restriction arises from the fact that we view
this concentration inequality as a multiplicative analogue of the Turán–Kubilius in-
equality, which asserts that, on average, f is close to its mean over a prescribed set
of integers. Since the mean value of f is expected to be close to that of χnit , it is
convenient, in order to retain the information carried by χ, to restrict the summa-
tion to arithmetic progressions of the form n = Qm + a, where the modulus of χ
divides Q.
