1. Introduction
A. Beurling [2] defined a system of generalized primes P as an unbounded sequence (pj )j≥1
satisfying 1 < p1 ≤ p2 ≤ . . . . The corresponding system of generalized integers N is the
multiplicative semigroup generated by 1 and P. With these systems one associates functions
π(x) and N(x) counting the number of primes, respectively integers, not exceeding x, taking
multiplicities into account for those integers having multiple representations as a product of
generalized primes.
A substantial part of the theory of Beurling generalized primes focuses on the asymptotic
behavior of the counting functions π and N and how one is related to the other. This was in
fact Beurling’s main motivation; in his seminal paper [2], he showed the following abstract
form of the prime number theorem (PNT). Suppose that for some a > 0 and γ > 3/2,
N(x) = ax + O x log−γ x . Then π(x) ∼ x/ log x holds. In the same article, it was also
shown that the condition γ > 3/2 is sharp (see also [5]). The first abstract PNT with
remainder actually predates Beurling’s paper in some sense, since the methods of Landau’s
work on the prime ideal theorem [11], cast into the language of Beurling generalized primes,
yield the following theorem (PNT with de la Vallée Poussin remainder). Suppose that
N(x) = ax + O(xθ ) holds for some

Debruyne and J. Vindas in [3]. In an upcoming article, the same authors will show that
the theorem of Diamond is optimal for every 0 < α ≤ 1. In the converse direction, the
value for α was improved to α = β/7.91 by Hall in [8]. (A slight refinement of his argument
actually yields α = β/(β +6.91), se e.g. [7, Section 16.4].) Hall’s proof consists of a Tauberian
argument combined with bounds on the zeta function which are obtained via a generalization
of the familiar “3-4-1-inequality”. The value 6.91 arises from a specific choice of a positive
trigonometric polynomial1.
Let us denote by β ∗ (α) the supremum of all β for which (Nβ ) follows from (Pα ), and
similarly α∗ (β) as the supremum of all α for which (Pα ) follows from (Nβ ). The aforementioned results imply that β ∗ (1) = 1/2, β ∗ (α) ≥ α/(α + 1) for 0 < α < 1, α∗ (1) = 1/2
and α∗ (β) ≥ β/(β + 6.91) for 0 < β < 1. Based on Landau’s PNT and the exponent
β = α/(α + 1) in the converse problem, it was conjectured2 by Bateman and Diamond [1]
that α∗ (β) ≥ β/(β + 1).
