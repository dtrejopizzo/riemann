\begin{abstract}
This note goes step by step, with numerically generated plots, through the
relation between two objects that at first sight have nothing to do with each
other: the \emph{prime numbers} (which live on the real axis) and the
\emph{nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function} (which live on the vertical
line $\mathrm{Re}(s)=1/2$). The guiding thread is an analogy with the
\textbf{Barkhausen oscillation condition}: zeros on the line $1/2$ describe a
system in \emph{marginal stability}, which neither diverges nor dies out but
oscillates forever. We close with \textbf{Riemann's explicit formula}, which shows
how the zeros literally ``reconstruct'' the primes.
\end{abstract}

\tableofcontents

\section{Starting point: even numbers and primes}

We start with the two most basic sequences.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Even numbers:} generated by the formula $2n$. Sequence:
  $2,4,6,8,\dots$ All are multiples of $2$; a perfectly regular progression.
  \item \textbf{Primes:} integers greater than $1$ divisible only by $1$ and
  themselves. Sequence: $2,3,5,7,11,13,\dots$ Except for $2$ (the only even
  prime), all are odd. Their spacing is irregular.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:sucesiones} plots the first $500$ terms of each, with the
position $n$ on the horizontal axis and the value on the vertical one.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{sucesiones.png}
  \caption{First $500$ even numbers (left) and primes (right). The even numbers
  form a perfect line ($2n$); the primes a curve that slowly bends upward.}
  \label{fig:sucesiones}
\end{figure}

\section{Enlarging the scale: 10,000 terms}

With $10{,}000$ terms (Figure~\ref{fig:diez_mil}) the difference grows: the
$10{,}000$th even number is $20{,}000$, while the $10{,}000$th prime is
$104{,}729$, more than five times larger at the same position. The curvature of
the primes, which reflects the \textbf{prime number theorem} ($p_n \sim n\ln n$),
becomes evident.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{sucesiones_10000.png}
  \caption{First $10{,}000$ even numbers and primes, each on its own scale.}
  \label{fig:diez_mil}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The effect of a shared scale}

Plotted separately, each sequence uses its own vertical scale, which visually
``straightens'' the primes. On the \emph{same axes} (Figure~\ref{fig:comparacion})
the primes grow much faster than the even numbers. Two theoretical approximations
are added:

\begin{align}
  p_n &\approx n\ln n && \text{(simple approximation, falls short)}\\
  p_n &\approx n(\ln n + \ln\ln n) && \text{(improved approximation, overshoots)}
\end{align}

At $n=10{,}000$: the actual prime is $104{,}729$, while $n\ln n = 92{,}103$
($-12\%$) and $n(\ln n + \ln\ln n) = 114{,}307$ ($+9\%$). The actual value lies
\emph{between} the two; the third-order term $-n$ of Cesàro's expansion almost
exactly corrects the difference.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{sucesiones_comparacion.png}
  \caption{Even numbers, primes and the two theoretical approximations on the
  same axes. The blue line (even numbers) is flattened at the bottom; the primes
  (red) are enclosed by the green and violet curves.}
  \label{fig:comparacion}
\end{figure}

\section{The prime-counting function: $\pi(x)$ against $\mathrm{Li}(x)$}

To connect the primes with the zeta function we need the counting function
$\pi(x)$ = number of primes $\le x$, and its best smooth approximation, the
\textbf{logarithmic integral}:
\[
  \mathrm{Li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}.
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:pi_li} (top panel) shows that $\mathrm{Li}(x)$ follows $\pi(x)$
almost perfectly, while the simple approximation $x/\ln x$ falls short. The
important part is the bottom panel: the \textbf{error} $\pi(x)-\mathrm{Li}(x)$
oscillates irregularly and stays \emph{trapped} inside a band growing like
$\sqrt{x}$.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{pi_vs_li.png}
  \caption{Top: $\pi(x)$, $\mathrm{Li}(x)$ and $x/\ln x$. Bottom: the error
  $\pi(x)-\mathrm{Li}(x)$ oscillating inside a band $\sim\sqrt{x}$.}
  \label{fig:pi_li}
\end{figure}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{This is where $1/2$ appears.} The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to
this error never escaping the band $\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}$ (up to logarithms). That
exponent $\tfrac12$ is \emph{the same} $\tfrac12$ as the critical line. If a
single zero were at $\mathrm{Re}(s)=0.6$, the error would grow like $x^{0.6}$
and break the band.
\end{quote}

\section{The Barkhausen analogy}

\subsection{Zeros as modes of an oscillator}

The \textbf{explicit formula} (Section~\ref{sec:explicita}) writes the prime
count as a sum of terms $x^\rho$, one for each zero $\rho=\beta+i\gamma$. With
$x=e^u$ (that is, $u=\ln x$):
\[
  x^\rho = x^\beta \cdot x^{i\gamma}
         = \underbrace{e^{\beta u}}_{\text{envelope}}\cdot
           \underbrace{e^{i\gamma u}}_{\text{oscillation}}.
\]

This is exactly a \emph{mode} $e^{\sigma t}e^{i\omega t}$ of a linear system,
with growth rate $\sigma=\beta$ and frequency $\omega=\gamma$. Normalising by the
dominant term $\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}$:
\[
  \frac{x^\beta}{x^{1/2}} = x^{\beta-1/2} = e^{(\beta-\frac12)u}.
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \hline
  Position of the zero & Regime \\
  \hline
  $\beta > 1/2$ & growing envelope $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{diverges} (unstable)\\
  $\beta < 1/2$ & decaying envelope $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{dies} (overdamped)\\
  $\beta = 1/2$ & constant envelope $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{sustained oscillation}\\
  \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The condition $\beta=1/2$ is exactly the \textbf{Barkhausen condition} of an
oscillator: loop gain of modulus $1$, neither growth nor decay.
Figure~\ref{fig:barkhausen} illustrates it with the frequency of the first zero,
$\gamma=14.1347\ldots$

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{barkhausen_riemann.png}
  \caption{The mode $e^{(\beta-1/2)u}\cos(\gamma u)$ for three positions of the
  zero. Only $\beta=1/2$ (green) has a flat envelope: sustained oscillation.}
  \label{fig:barkhausen}
\end{figure}

\subsection{What oscillates: a phasor of modulus 1}

The oscillation between $+1$ and $-1$ is the projection (real part) of a phasor
$e^{i\gamma u}=\cos(\gamma u)+i\sin(\gamma u)$ turning on the unit circle. Its
modulus is \emph{exactly} $1$:
\[
  \bigl|e^{i\gamma u}\bigr| = 1.
\]
This unit modulus \emph{is} the Barkhausen loop gain, and it holds only when
$\beta=1/2$. The amplitude neither grows nor dies because, on the marginal edge,
energy per cycle is conserved, as in an ideal $LC$ oscillator without resistance.

\section{Zeta as a waveform}

On the critical line, the Riemann--Siegel function $Z(t)$ is \emph{real} and
satisfies $|Z(t)|=|\zeta(\tfrac12+it)|$. It is literally the waveform of the zeta
oscillator: it crosses zero exactly at each zero
$\gamma=14.13,\,21.02,\,25.01,\dots$ (Figure~\ref{fig:zeta}, top panel).

The bottom panel compares $|\zeta(\sigma+it)|$ for $\sigma=0.3,\,0.5,\,0.7$.
\textbf{Only at $\sigma=1/2$ does the curve touch zero} in this range (minimum
$0.001$ against $0.17$ and $0.15$ on the other lines).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{zeta_barkhausen.png}
  \caption{Top: $Z(t)$ crosses zero at each zeta zero. Bottom: only
  $\sigma=1/2$ makes $|\zeta|$ touch zero (Barkhausen condition).}
  \label{fig:zeta}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Behaviour at large height ($t$ up to $100{,}000$)}

Up to $t=100{,}000$ there are about $138{,}000$ zeros, too many to trace the full
wave. Figure~\ref{fig:cien_mil} shows the global pattern (the count $N(t)$ and
the mean spacing $2\pi/\ln(t/2\pi)$) together with four zoom windows of the
actual wave. Key observation: \textbf{the wave remains marginal at every height}:
it oscillates across zero, neither converging nor diverging, while the frequency
slowly increases (the spacing drops from $2.27$ at $t\approx100$ to $0.65$ at
$t\approx100{,}000$).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{zeta_barkhausen_100k.png}
  \caption{Global pattern up to $t=100{,}000$ (top row) and windows of the wave
  $Z(t)$ at increasing heights (bottom row). Marginality persists over the
  $138{,}000$ oscillations.}
  \label{fig:cien_mil}
\end{figure}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{That the curves neither converge nor diverge is the key point.} On the
line $1/2$ we stand exactly on the marginal edge; the envelope is flat by
construction. Convergence or divergence would appear only off the line.
\end{quote}

\section{Two populations, two lines}

It is tempting to think that the primes ``fall'' on the line $1/2$. They do not:
the primes (and the even numbers) are \emph{real numbers} and live on the
horizontal axis. The zeta zeros are \emph{complex numbers} $\tfrac12\pm i\gamma$
and live on the vertical line $\mathrm{Re}(s)=1/2$. Figure~\ref{fig:plano} shows
them together: two perpendicular rows of points.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{ceros_vs_pares_plano.png}
  \caption{The first $20$ zeta zeros (red, on the vertical line $1/2$) and the
  first $20$ even numbers (blue, on the real axis). Objects of different nature,
  in perpendicular directions of the plane.}
  \label{fig:plano}
\end{figure}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \hline
   & Even numbers & Zeta zeros \\
  \hline
  Nature & real & complex \\
  Where they live & real axis (horizontal) & line $1/2$ (vertical) \\
  Spacing & regular (trivial) & irregular (deep) \\
  Formula & $2n$, exact & no closed formula \\
  \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{The explicit formula: the zeros reconstruct the primes}
\label{sec:explicita}

How can a list of points on a vertical line be related to the primes, which live
on the real axis? The answer is \textbf{Riemann's explicit formula}. With the
Chebyshev step function $\psi(x)$, which jumps by $\ln p$ at each prime power
$p^k$:
\[
  \psi(x) = x - \sum_{\rho} \frac{x^\rho}{\rho} - \ln(2\pi)
            - \tfrac12\ln\!\left(1-x^{-2}\right).
\]

Each zero $\rho=\tfrac12+i\gamma$ contributes a wave
$x^\rho=\sqrt{x}\,e^{i\gamma\ln x}$ of frequency $\gamma$. Adding more and more
waves, the reconstruction approaches the jagged staircase of the primes
(Figure~\ref{fig:explicita}): with $1$ zero, a smooth wave; with $50$ zeros, the
steps appear \emph{exactly} at $2,3,4,5,7,8,9,11,\dots$

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{formula_explicita.png}
  \caption{The red staircase $\psi(x)$ encodes the primes. The blue curves
  reconstruct it using only the zeta zeros: the more zeros, the sharper the
  steps.}
  \label{fig:explicita}
\end{figure}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Musical analogy.} The prime staircase is a chord; the zeta zeros are the
pure frequencies (the notes) that produce it when added. The primes are not
\emph{on} the line $1/2$: they are \emph{encoded by} the points of the line
$1/2$. The vertical line is the frequency spectrum; the real axis is where the
music sounds.
\end{quote}

Since $|x^\rho|=x^{\mathrm{Re}(\rho)}$, all waves on the critical line have the
same amplitude $\sqrt{x}$. That balance (no note louder than another) is what
keeps the primes as regular as possible. It is, once more, the Barkhausen
condition: all waves marginal, none dominating.

\section{Closing}

The whole tour (even numbers $\to$ primes $\to$ $\pi(x)$ vs $\mathrm{Li}(x)$
$\to$ zeros $\to$ Barkhausen $\to$ explicit formula) shows that the Riemann
hypothesis is a statement about primes \emph{disguised} as a statement about
zeros. In oscillator language:

\begin{quote}
\emph{The zeta function would maintain the marginal-stability (Barkhausen)
condition for its infinitely many zeros, exactly and without any visible control
mechanism.} The open question is which self-adjoint operator, in the spirit of
Hilbert--Pólya, would act as the ``automatic gain control'' that pins every zero
to $\mathrm{Re}(s)=1/2$.
\end{quote}
