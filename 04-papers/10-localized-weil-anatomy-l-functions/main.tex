\title[The localized-Weil anatomy of $L$-functions]{The localized-Weil anatomy of
$L$-functions: Euler products, Satake power sums, and Rankin--Selberg products}
\author{David Alejandro Trejo Pizzo}
\thanks{Independent Researcher. \texttt{dtrejopizzo@gmail.com}}


\begin{document}
\begin{abstract}
To a Dirichlet series $L$ with a functional equation of Selberg type we attach a
per-prime-power \emph{anatomy} $\cR_L$, the ground-state-weighted profile of its
localized Weil form on a Hermite--Gauss window, whose sum over $n$ enters the
bottom eigenvalue. We show that an Euler product forces the anatomy to vanish
off prime powers, and conversely, whenever the ground-state autocorrelation does
not vanish at the relevant lags (for example across two suitable windows); for a
ground state close to the Gaussian, non-vanishing holds on an explicit range of
lags. The autocorrelation of a window function is a polynomial times a Gaussian,
and for pure Hermite modes it is given in closed form by a Laguerre polynomial.
When $\cR_L(p^k)$ and $c_0(\log p^k)$ are known, the anatomy determines the
Satake power sums, and hence the local factors, by standard symmetric-function
identities. These power sums behave multiplicatively under Rankin--Selberg
products and additively under isobaric sums. In degree one, combined with the
Kaczorowski--Perelli classification, the converse identifies $\zeta$ and the
$L$-functions of real primitive Dirichlet characters. A short numerical appendix checks the Laguerre formula
for pure Hermite modes. Nothing here concerns the location of zeros.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The localized Weil form~\cite{RP7} attaches to an $L$-function a quadratic form
$Q_L=\fa-\fp_L$ on a Hermite--Gauss window --- the archimedean Gram minus the
prime Gram. For a rank-one model of such forms, \cite{RP9} writes the bottom
eigenvalue as an archimedean term minus a sum of per-prime profiles, the
\emph{anatomy}; here we use the same decomposition for $Q_L$ itself, indexed by
all $n\ge2$. It is natural to ask whether the existence of an Euler product is
detected by the locality of this anatomy. We show that the forward implication always holds and
that the converse holds under an explicit non-vanishing hypothesis on the
ground-state autocorrelation. We then record what the anatomy determines for
standard $\GL(n)$ $L$-functions. None of this concerns the location of the
zeros.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Results.}
\begin{itemize}
\item[(I)] \emph{Characterization} (Theorem~\ref{thm:char}): an Euler product
implies factorizing anatomy; conversely, factorizing anatomy implies an Euler
product under the non-vanishing hypotheses of Theorems~\ref{thm:convdet}
and~\ref{thm:conv2}.
\item[(II)] \emph{Satake power sums} (Theorem~\ref{thm:satake}): for self-dual cuspidal $\pi$ on $\GL(n)$,
where $c_0(\log p^k)\ne0$, the anatomy determines the power sums of the Satake
parameters, hence the local factor, the number of distinct nonzero parameters,
and $\max_i|\alpha_{i,p}|$.
\item[(III)] \emph{Functorial operations} (Theorems~\ref{thm:rs}--\ref{thm:lift}):
the power sums multiply under Rankin--Selberg products and are polynomial in the
base power sums for lifts (standard facts, restated in the anatomy's terms).
\item[(IV)] \emph{Degree one} (Theorem~\ref{thm:gl1}, \S\ref{sec:auto2}):
combined with~\cite{KP1}, factorizing anatomy in two windows identifies $\zeta$ and the
$L$-functions of real primitive Dirichlet characters.
\end{itemize}

\section{The localized Weil form and its anatomy}\label{sec:setup}
Let $L(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}a_L(n)n^{-s}$ lie in the class $\cS^\#$ of
Selberg~\cite{Sel} and Kaczorowski--Perelli~\cite{KP1}: a Dirichlet series,
absolutely convergent for $\mathrm{Re}\,s>1$, with $a_L(1)=1$, a meromorphic
continuation with at most a pole at $s=1$, of finite order, and a functional
equation of the standard shape with factor
$Q^s\prod_j\Gamma(\lambda_js+\mu_j)$, $Q>0$, $\lambda_j>0$; its degree is $d_L=2\sum_j\lambda_j$. We do
\emph{not} assume an Euler product. We assume throughout that
\[
-\frac{L'}{L}(s)=\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda_L(n)}{n^{s}}
\]
converges absolutely for $\mathrm{Re}\,s>1$, with $|\Lambda_L(n)|\ll n^{A}$ for
some $A$ (this holds, for instance, when $L$ has an Euler product satisfying
the axioms of $\cS$). Throughout, $\Lambda_L(n)$ is assumed real (for instance
$a_L(n)\in\mathbb R$), and the multiset of pairs $(\lambda_j,\mu_j)$ is assumed stable under $\mu_j\mapsto\overline{\mu_j}$ (as holds for $\zeta$, real Dirichlet characters and self-dual $\pi$), so that $\Omega_L$ below is even. Then, for
$\mathrm{Re}\,s$ large, $\log L(s)=\sum_{n\ge2}\Lambda_L(n)n^{-s}/\log n$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{The window.} Fix a width $\sigma>0$ and a dimension
$d\ge1$, and let
\[
W=W_{\sigma,d}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb C}\bigl\{H_j(t/\sigma)e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}:
0\le j<d\bigr\}\subset L^2(\mathbb R),
\]
with $H_j$ the physicists' Hermite polynomials; thus $W$ consists of the
functions $P(t)e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$ with $\deg P<d$. On $W$ the localized Weil
form is
\[
Q_L[g]=\fa[g]-\fp_L[g],\qquad
\fp_L[g]=2\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda_L(n)}{\sqrt n}\,\mathrm{Re}\,(g\star\tilde g)(\log n),
\]
with $\fa[g]=\int\Omega_L(r)|\widehat g(r)|^2\,dr$, with $\widehat g(r)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int g(t)e^{-irt}\,dt$ (plus the polar term when
$L$ has a pole at $s=1$), where $\Omega_L(r)=2\log Q+2\,\mathrm{Re}\sum_j
\lambda_j\psi\bigl(\lambda_j(\tfrac12+ir)+\mu_j\bigr)$ is the archimedean
multiplier of $L$ (for $\zeta$, $\Omega(r)=\mathrm{Re}\,
\psi(\tfrac14+\tfrac{ir}2)-\log\pi$, as in~\cite{RP7}). The results below use
only that $\fa$ is a Hermitian form on $W$ which is real on real functions. Also
\[
(g\star\tilde g)(u)=\int g(t)\overline{g(t-u)}\,dt=\langle g,\tau_ug\rangle,
\qquad(\tau_ug)(t)=g(t-u),
\]
the autocorrelation, whose Fourier transform is $|\widehat g|^2\ge0$. The series
defining $\fp_L$ converges absolutely on $W$ by the Gaussian decay of
$g\star\tilde g$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:auto} and the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite}). Since $\Lambda_L$ is real and $W$ is
finite-dimensional, $Q_L$ is a Hermitian form on $W$, real symmetric in the real
Hermite basis, and has a bottom eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}(Q_L)$; a
\emph{ground state} is a unit vector $g_0\in W$ with
$Q_L[g_0]=\lambda_{\min}(Q_L)$. It exists; it is unique up to a phase when
$\lambda_{\min}$ is a simple eigenvalue, and otherwise we fix a choice. We write
$c_0=g_0\star\tilde g_0$.

\begin{definition}[Anatomy]\label{def:anat}
The \emph{anatomy} of $L$ (in the window $W$, with the chosen ground state
$g_0$) is the per-$n$ profile
\[
\cR_L(n)=\frac{2\Lambda_L(n)}{\sqrt n}\,c_0(\log n),\qquad\text{so
that}\qquad
\lambda_{\min}(Q_L)=R_\infty-\sum_{n\ge2}\cR_L(n),\quad R_\infty:=\fa[g_0],
\]
the identity holding by definition of $\fp_L$ (the analogue for $Q_L$ of the
anatomy identity proved in~\cite[Prop.~3.1]{RP9} for a model form). We say $L$ has \emph{factorizing anatomy} if $\cR_L(n)=0$ for
every $n$ with at least two distinct prime factors.
\end{definition}

We take $g_0$ real, as we may since $Q_L$ is real; then $c_0$ is real and even,
and $\cR_L(n)=2\Lambda_L(n)n^{-1/2}c_0(\log n)$. The analysis below turns on the
interplay between the arithmetic factor $\Lambda_L(n)$ and the analytic factor
$c_0(\log n)$.

\section{The forward direction}\label{sec:fwd}
\begin{theorem}[Euler $\Rightarrow$ factorizing]\label{thm:fwd}
If $L\in\cS^\#$ has an Euler product $L(s)=\prod_p L_p(p^{-s})$, then $\Lambda_L$ is
supported on prime powers; consequently $\cR_L(n)=0$ for every non-prime-power
$n$, and the anatomy factorizes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The Euler product gives $\log L(s)=\sum_p\log L_p(p^{-s})$, hence
\[
-\frac{L'}{L}(s)=\sum_p(\log p)\,p^{-s}\,\frac{L_p'}{L_p}(p^{-s}),
\]
a sum of
per-prime Dirichlet series in the single variable $p^{-s}$. Comparing Dirichlet
coefficients, $\Lambda_L(n)=0$ unless $n=p^k$ is a prime power; for prime
powers, $\Lambda_L(p^k)$ is a polynomial in the coefficients of $L_p$ given by
the Newton--Girard identities. Since $\Lambda_L$ vanishes off prime powers, so
does $\cR_L(n)=2\Lambda_L(n)n^{-1/2}c_0(\log n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:fwd} identifies the footprint of an Euler product in the
localized Weil form: the prime form decomposes into single-prime contributions.
The question of the converse is whether this absence of mixed terms, read off
the \emph{spectral} profile $\cR_L$ rather than the coefficients $\Lambda_L$,
already forces multiplicativity.
\end{remark}

\section{The autocorrelation of the ground state}\label{sec:auto}
Because $\cR_L(n)=2\Lambda_L(n)n^{-1/2}c_0(\log n)$, the anatomy
vanishes at a non-prime-power $n$ if and only if $\Lambda_L(n)=0$ \emph{or}
$c_0(\log n)=0$. The converse therefore turns on the real zeros
of $c_0$.

\begin{lemma}[Zero structure]\label{lem:auto}
Let $g_0(t)=P(t)\,e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$ with $P$ a real polynomial of degree $m$
(every real element of $W$ has this form with $m<d$). Then
\[
c_0(u)=q_\sigma(u)\,e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2},\qquad q_\sigma\ \text{a polynomial},\
\deg q_\sigma\le 2m,
\]
so $c_0$ has at most $2m$ real zeros (note $q_\sigma(0)=\|g_0\|^2>0$). For the lowest
mode ($m=0$, $P=1$), $c_0(u)=\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma\,e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}>0$ has none.
For a pure Hermite mode $g_0=H_m(t/\sigma)e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$,
\[
c_0(u)=2^m m!\,\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma\,L_m\!\bigl(u^2/2\sigma^2\bigr)e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2},
\]
with $L_m$ the Laguerre polynomial; since $c_0$ is even, it has exactly $2m$
real zeros $u=\pm\sigma\sqrt{2\rho_j}$, where $0<\rho_1<\dots<\rho_m$ are the
roots of $L_m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $c_0(u)=\int P(t)P(t-u)e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}e^{-(t-u)^2/2\sigma^2}\,dt$. The
exponent is $-\frac1{2\sigma^2}(t^2+(t-u)^2)=-\frac1{\sigma^2}(t-\tfrac u2)^2
-\frac{u^2}{4\sigma^2}$. Substituting $s=t-\tfrac u2$,
\[
c_0(u)=e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}\int P(s+\tfrac u2)P(s-\tfrac u2)e^{-s^2/\sigma^2}\,ds .
\]
$P(s+u/2)P(s-u/2)$ is a polynomial of total degree $2m$ in $(s,u)$; integrating
each monomial $s^au^b$ against $e^{-s^2/\sigma^2}$ gives a constant times $u^b$
with $b\le2m$. Hence the integral is a polynomial $q_\sigma(u)$ of degree
$\le2m$. For $m=0$ the integral is $\sqrt\pi\,\sigma$. For the Hermite case put
$s=\sigma x$ and $v=u/2\sigma$: the integral is
$\sigma\int H_m(x+v)H_m(x-v)e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sigma\,2^mm!\sqrt\pi\,L_m(2v^2)$ by the
classical formula $\int e^{-x^2}H_m(x+y)H_m(x+z)\,dx=2^mm!\sqrt\pi\,L_m(-2yz)$
\cite[7.377]{GR}, and $2v^2=u^2/2\sigma^2$. The roots of $L_m$ are simple and
positive, which gives the zeros.
\end{proof}

\section{The converse and the characterization}\label{sec:conv}
\begin{theorem}[Converse in a single window]\label{thm:convdet}
\textup{(a)} Assume the ground state $g_0$ satisfies $c_0(\log n)\neq0$
for every non-prime-power $n\ge2$. Then factorizing anatomy implies
$\Lambda_L(n)=0$ off prime powers, hence
\[
\log L(s)=\sum_p\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\Lambda_L(p^k)}{k\log p}\,p^{-ks},
\]
and $L$ has a (formal, absolutely convergent for $\mathrm{Re}\,s>1$) Euler product
\[
L(s)=\prod_p\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\Lambda_L(p^k)}{k\log p}\,p^{-ks}\Bigr).
\]

\textup{(b)} Let $\gamma(t)=(\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$ be the
normalized Gaussian, so $\gamma\star\tilde\gamma(u)=e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}$. If
$\|g_0-\gamma\|\le\varepsilon$, then
$|c_0(u)-\gamma\star\tilde\gamma(u)|\le2\varepsilon$ for all $u$;
hence, for $\varepsilon<1/3$, $c_0(u)>0$ whenever
$|u|<2\sigma\sqrt{\log(1/(3\varepsilon))}$. Consequently, if the anatomy
factorizes, then $\Lambda_L(n)=0$ for every non-prime-power $n$ with
$\log n<2\sigma\sqrt{\log(1/(3\varepsilon))}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) If $n$ is not a prime power, $\cR_L(n)=0$ and $c_0(\log n)\ne0$
give $\Lambda_L(n)=0$. Then, for $\mathrm{Re}\,s$ large, $\log L(s)=\sum_n
\Lambda_L(n)n^{-s}/\log n$ involves only $n=p^k$, with $\log n=k\log p$, which is
the displayed formula; it converges absolutely for $\mathrm{Re}\,s>1$ because
$\sum|\Lambda_L(n)|n^{-\mathrm{Re}\,s}$ does. Grouping by primes and
exponentiating gives the product.

(b) With $h=g_0-\gamma$ and $\|\tau_u\|=1$,
\[
c_0(u)-\gamma\star\tilde\gamma(u)=\langle h,\tau_ug_0\rangle+\langle\gamma,\tau_uh\rangle,
\]
whose modulus is at most $\varepsilon\|g_0\|+\varepsilon=2\varepsilon$
(recall $\|g_0\|=\|\gamma\|=1$). Thus $c_0(u)\ge e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}-2\varepsilon>0$ whenever
$e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}>2\varepsilon$, in particular in the stated range, where
$e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}>3\varepsilon$. The last assertion follows as in (a).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Converse, two-window form]\label{thm:conv2}
Suppose there exist widths $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ (with windows $W_{\sigma_1,d}$,
$W_{\sigma_2,d}$ and chosen ground states) such that, for each non-prime-power
$n$, $c_0^{(\sigma_1)}(\log n)$ and $c_0^{(\sigma_2)}(\log n)$
are not both zero. If the anatomy factorizes in both windows, then $L$ has an
Euler product in the sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:convdet}\textup{(a)}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a non-prime-power $n$, one of the two values
$c_0^{(\sigma_i)}(\log n)$ is nonzero, and $\cR_L^{(\sigma_i)}(n)=0$
then forces $\Lambda_L(n)=0$. Conclude as in Theorem~\ref{thm:convdet}(a).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Characterization]\label{thm:char}
If $L\in\cS^\#$ has an Euler product, its anatomy factorizes in every window
(Theorem~\ref{thm:fwd}). Conversely, if the anatomy factorizes in a window whose
ground state satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:convdet}\textup{(a)},
or in two windows satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:conv2}, then $L$
has an Euler product.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
The ground state, and hence the zeros of $c_0$, depend on $\sigma$ through $Q_L$,
so whether the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:conv2} can be met is a property of
$L$ that we do not establish here. By Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}, each $c_0$ has at
most $2(d-1)$ real zeros, so the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:convdet}(a) can
fail at no more than $d-1$ positive lags $\log n$. For pure Hermite modes the
zeros scale linearly with $\sigma$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}; see also
Appendix~\ref{app:code}), which suggests, but does not prove, that a
coincidental zero at a fixed $\log n$ does not persist as the window varies.
\end{remark}

\section{\texorpdfstring{Positivity as a conjugated operator inequality, and concavity}{Positivity as a conjugated operator inequality, and concavity}}
\label{sec:carleson}

We record what the anatomy says about the \emph{positivity} of the localized
form. In this section assume that $\fa$ is positive definite on $W$ (this
depends on the window; for $\zeta$, $\Omega$ takes negative values near $r=0$), and
factor $\fa[g]=\|Bg\|^2$ with $B\colon W\to W$ invertible (for instance
$B=A^{1/2}$, with $A$ the positive operator of $\fa$).

\begin{theorem}[Conjugated form of local positivity]\label{thm:carleson}
$\lambda_{\min}(Q_L)\ge0$ if and only if
\[
2\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda_L(n)}{\sqrt n}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\langle B^{-1}h,\tau_{\log n}
B^{-1}h\rangle\le\|h\|^2\qquad\text{for all }h\in W.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\lambda_{\min}\ge0\iff\fp_L[g]\le\fa[g]=\|Bg\|^2$ for all $g\in W$. Substitute
$g=B^{-1}h$ and use $(g\star\tilde g)(u)=\langle g,\tau_ug\rangle$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Absolute convergence]\label{prop:finite}
For $u\in\mathbb R$ let $\|\tau_u\|_B=\sup_{h\ne0}|\langle B^{-1}h,\tau_uB^{-1}h
\rangle|/\|h\|^2$. There is a polynomial $M_d$ (depending on $d$ only) with
$\|\tau_u\|_B\le\|B^{-1}\|^2M_d(u/\sigma)e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}$. Consequently, if
$|\Lambda_L(n)|\ll n^{A}$ for some $A$ (for instance
$|\Lambda_L(p^k)|\le N\log p$ for the standard $L$-function of $\GL(N)$ under the
Ramanujan conjecture), then $\sum_n|\Lambda_L(n)|n^{-1/2}\|\tau_{\log n}\|_B<\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\varphi_0,\dots,\varphi_{d-1}$ be the orthonormal Hermite functions spanning
$W$, so $\varphi_j=c_jH_j(t/\sigma)e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$. By the computation in the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:auto} (with $P(s+u/2)P(s-u/2)$ replaced by
$H_j((s+u/2)/\sigma)H_k((s-u/2)/\sigma)$), each entry
$\langle\varphi_j,\tau_u\varphi_k\rangle$ equals $p_{jk}(u/\sigma)e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}$
with $p_{jk}$ a polynomial of degree $\le j+k$ independent of $\sigma$. For
$g=\sum_ja_j\varphi_j$, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
$|\langle g,\tau_ug\rangle|\le\|g\|^2(\sum_{j,k}|p_{jk}(u/\sigma)|^2)^{1/2}
e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2}$; take $M_d=(\sum_{j,k}|p_{jk}|^2)^{1/2}$ bounded by a polynomial,
and $g=B^{-1}h$, $\|g\|\le\|B^{-1}\|\|h\|$. Since
$e^{-(\log n)^2/4\sigma^2}$ decays faster than any power of $n$, the series
converges.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Each form $g\mapsto\mathrm{Re}\langle g,\tau_ug\rangle$ is indefinite in general
(its values on unit vectors range over a subset of $[-1,1]$), so
Theorem~\ref{thm:carleson} is an operator inequality for an indefinite family,
not a Carleson-measure or Bessel condition.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Concavity]\label{thm:concave}
Regard $Q_L=Q(\Lambda)$ as depending on the coefficient vector
$\Lambda=(\Lambda_n)_{2\le n\le N}$ (finitely many, the others fixed). The map
$\Lambda\mapsto\lambda_{\min}(Q(\Lambda))$ is concave; hence its minimum over a
box $\prod_n[\ell_n,c_n]$ is attained at a vertex.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\lambda_{\min}(Q(\Lambda))=\min_{\|g\|=1}Q(\Lambda)[g]$ and each
$Q(\Lambda)[g]$ is affine in $\Lambda$; a minimum of affine functions is concave.
A concave function on a polytope attains its minimum at an extreme point.
\end{proof}

Whether the vertex values are positive is not addressed here. Compare the
concavity statement of~\cite{RP9} for the model form.

\section{\texorpdfstring{$\GL(n)$: Satake power sums and local factors}{GL(n): Satake power sums and local factors}}\label{sec:gln}
Let $L(s,\pi)$ be the standard $L$-function of a cuspidal automorphic
self-dual representation $\pi$ of $\GL(n)$ over $\mathbb Q$ (so that
$s_k(p)\in\mathbb R$ below), with local factors
$L_p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^n(1-\alpha_{i,p}x)^{-1}$ and Satake parameters
$\{\alpha_{i,p}\}_{i=1}^n$ (some may vanish at ramified $p$). Then
$\Lambda_L(p^k)=(\log p)\,s_k(p)$ with the \emph{$k$-th power sum}
\[
s_k(p)=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_{i,p}^k,\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\cR_L(p^k)=\frac{2\log p}{p^{k/2}}\,s_k(p)\,c_0(\log p^k).
\]

\begin{theorem}[Satake power sums]\label{thm:satake}
Fix $p$ and assume $c_0(\log p^k)\ne0$ for all $k\ge1$. Then the anatomy
$\{\cR_L(p^k)\}_{k\ge1}$ determines:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the power sums $s_k(p)$ for all $k$, by dividing out the explicit factor
$2p^{-k/2}(\log p)c_0(\log p^k)$;
\item $\rank\bigl[s_{i+j+1}(p)\bigr]_{0\le i,j\le N}$ $(N\ge n)$, which equals the
number of distinct nonzero $\alpha_{i,p}$; it equals $n$ when they are distinct
and nonzero;
\item the local factor $L_p$, since $L_p(x)=\exp\bigl(\sum_{k\ge1}s_k(p)x^k/k\bigr)$
(equivalently, when $n$ is known, the first $n$ power sums fix the elementary
symmetric functions by Newton--Girard);
\item $\max_i|\alpha_{i,p}|=\limsup_{k\to\infty}|s_k(p)|^{1/k}$; hence
$\max_i|\alpha_{i,p}|\le1$ if and only if
$\cR_L(p^k)p^{k/2}/c_0(\log p^k)$ grows subexponentially in $k$.
\end{enumerate}
If the hypothesis holds at all but finitely many $p$, then by strong
multiplicity one the anatomy at those primes determines $\pi$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) is immediate. (2): let $v_1,\dots,v_r$ be the distinct nonzero values among
the $\alpha_{i,p}$, with multiplicities $m_l\ge1$. Then
$s_{i+j+1}=\sum_lm_lv_l\,v_l^{i}v_l^{j}$, so
$[s_{i+j+1}]=V^{\top}DV$ with $V=[v_l^{j}]_{l\le r,\,0\le j\le N}$ and
$D=\mathrm{diag}(m_lv_l)$ invertible. For $N+1\ge r$ the Vandermonde $V$ has rank
$r$, so the product has rank $r$. (3): $\log L_p(x)=-\sum_i\log(1-\alpha_{i,p}x)
=\sum_ks_k(p)x^k/k$. (4): $\sum_{k\ge1}s_kx^k=\sum_lm_lv_lx/(1-v_lx)$ is a rational
function whose poles are exactly the $v_l^{-1}$ (the coefficients $m_lv_l$ are
nonzero), so its radius of convergence is $1/\max_l|v_l|$, and the
Cauchy--Hadamard formula gives $\limsup|s_k|^{1/k}=\max_l|v_l|$ (or $0$ if
$r=0$). Since $\cR_L(p^k)p^{k/2}/c_0(\log p^k)=2(\log p)s_k$, the last assertion
follows. Strong multiplicity one~\cite{JS} recovers $\pi$ from the local factors
at almost all $p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:char} concerns the \emph{existence} of an Euler product;
Theorem~\ref{thm:satake} shows that, where $c_0(\log p^k)\ne0$, the anatomy
contains the same information as the local factors. Its content beyond the
classical relation $\Lambda_L(p^k)=(\log p)s_k(p)$ is only the division by the
known factor $c_0(\log p^k)$.
\end{remark}

\section{The anatomy generating function and local reconstruction}\label{sec:gen}
The following identity is the classical logarithmic derivative of the Euler
factor, restated in terms of the power sums read off by
Theorem~\ref{thm:satake}(1).
\begin{theorem}[Local reconstruction]\label{thm:gen}
Let $G_p(x)=\sum_{k\ge1}s_k(p)\,x^k$. Then
\[
G_p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\alpha_{i,p}x}{1-\alpha_{i,p}x}=x\,\frac{L_p'(x)}{L_p(x)},
\qquad
L_p(x)=\exp\!\int_0^x\frac{G_p(t)}{t}\,dt .
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\sum_k s_k(p)x^k=\sum_i\sum_{k\ge1}(\alpha_{i,p}x)^k=\sum_i\alpha_{i,p}x/
(1-\alpha_{i,p}x)$. On the other hand $\log L_p(x)=-\sum_i\log(1-\alpha_{i,p}x)
=\sum_k s_k(p)x^k/k$, whence $x\,\tfrac{d}{dx}\log L_p=G_p$. Integrating recovers
$\log L_p$, and exponentiating $L_p$.
\end{proof}

\section{Functoriality of the power sums}\label{sec:funct}
The power-sum encoding is compatible with the standard functorial operations;
the statements below are standard facts about Satake parameters.

\begin{theorem}[Rankin--Selberg multiplicativity]\label{thm:rs}
Let $\pi,\pi'$ be automorphic representations of $\GL(n)$, $\GL(n')$ with Satake
parameters $\{\alpha_{i,p}\}$, $\{\beta_{j,p}\}$ at an unramified $p$. The
Rankin--Selberg $L$-function $L(s,\pi\times\pi')$ has local parameters
$\{\alpha_{i,p}\beta_{j,p}\}$, so its power sums factor:
\[
s_k(\pi\times\pi',p)=\Bigl(\sum_i\alpha_{i,p}^k\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_j\beta_{j,p}^k\Bigr)
=s_k(\pi,p)\,s_k(\pi',p).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The local parameters of $L(s,\pi\times\pi')$ at unramified $p$ are the
products $\alpha_{i,p}\beta_{j,p}$; then
$s_k=\sum_{i,j}(\alpha_{i,p}\beta_{j,p})^k$, which factors as stated.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Lifts are polynomial in the base power sums]\label{thm:lift}
Let $r$ be a polynomial representation of $\GL_n(\mathbb C)$ (e.g.\
$\Sym^m$, $\wedge^m$) and let $L_p(x,\pi,r)=\det(1-r(A_p)x)^{-1}$, with
$A_p=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{i,p})$, $p$ unramified. Its power sums are symmetric
polynomials in the $\alpha_{i,p}$, hence universal polynomials in
$s_1(\pi,p),\dots,s_n(\pi,p)$. For
$\GL(2)$ with parameters $\{\alpha,\beta\}$:
\[
s_k(\Sym^2,p)=s_{2k}(\pi,p)+(\alpha\beta)^k,\qquad
s_k(\wedge^2,p)=(\alpha\beta)^k,
\]
so $s_k(\Sym^2,p)=s_{2k}(\pi,p)+s_k(\wedge^2,p)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The eigenvalues of $r(A_p)$ are monomials in the $\alpha_{i,p}$ given by the
weights of $r$, so their power sums are symmetric polynomials in the
$\alpha_{i,p}$, hence (Newton--Girard) polynomials in $s_1,\dots,s_n$. The
$\GL(2)$ formulas are the cases $\Sym^2\colon\{\alpha^2,\alpha\beta,\beta^2\}$ and
$\wedge^2\colon\{\alpha\beta\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
For unramified $p$, the map $\pi\mapsto\bigl(s_k(\pi,p)\bigr)_{k\ge1}$ sends
isobaric sums to sums and Rankin--Selberg products to pointwise products of
sequences.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The Satake multiset of an isobaric sum $\pi\boxplus\pi'$ is the union of those of
$\pi$ and $\pi'$, so the power sums add; the second statement is
Theorem~\ref{thm:rs}.
\end{proof}

\section{\texorpdfstring{Degree one, and the degree-two question}{Degree one, and the degree-two question}}\label{sec:auto2}
\begin{theorem}[Degree one]\label{thm:gl1}
Let $L\in\cS^\#$ have degree $d_L=1$ and satisfy the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:conv2}, and assume moreover $a_L(n)\ll_\varepsilon n^{\varepsilon}$
and $\Lambda_L(n)\ll n^{\theta}\log n$ for some $\theta<1/2$. If its anatomy factorizes in both windows, then $L$ has an Euler
product and lies in the Selberg class $\cS$, and $L(s)=\zeta(s)$ or $L(s)=L(s,\chi)$ with $\chi$ a primitive real
Dirichlet character.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:conv2}, $\log L(s)=\sum_nb(n)n^{-s}$ with
$b(n)=\Lambda_L(n)/\log n$ supported on prime powers and $b(n)\ll n^{\theta}$,
$\theta<1/2$: this is the Euler product axiom of $\cS$. Together with the
Ramanujan bound and the axioms of $\cS^\#$, $L\in\cS$ with $d_L=1$, and the classification of Kaczorowski and Perelli~\cite{KP1}
(building on~\cite{CG} for $d<1$) gives $L(s)=L(s+i\theta',\chi)$ for a
primitive Dirichlet character $\chi$ and some $\theta'\in\mathbb R$. Since
$\Lambda_L(p)=\chi(p)p^{-i\theta'}\log p$ is real, $\chi(p)p^{-i\theta'}=\pm1$ for
every $p\nmid q$; as $\chi(p)$ is an $M$-th root of unity ($M$ the order of
$\chi$), $p^{2iM\theta'}=1$, i.e.\ $\theta'\log p\in(\pi/M)\mathbb Z$ for all
such $p$. If $\theta'\ne0$, two such primes would have rational ratio of
logarithms, which is impossible; so $\theta'=0$ and $\chi(p)=\pm1$ for all
$p\nmid q$, i.e.\ $\chi$ is real (trivial $\chi$ giving $\zeta$).
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{The degree-two question.} For degree $2$, under the hypotheses
of Theorem~\ref{thm:char}, factorizing anatomy yields an Euler product, and,
where $c_0(\log p^k)\ne0$, Theorem~\ref{thm:satake}(2) reads off the number of
distinct nonzero local parameters. The conjecture that every degree-two element
of $\cS$ is automorphic is open. Kaczorowski and Perelli~\cite{KP7} proved that there are
no elements of $\cS$ with degree in $(1,2)$. The power-sum identities of
\S\ref{sec:gen}--\S\ref{sec:funct} express $\Sym^2$ and $\wedge^2$ data in terms
of the base anatomy.

\section{Conclusion}
The localized-Weil anatomy vanishes off prime powers whenever $L$ has an Euler
product (Theorem~\ref{thm:fwd}); conversely, factorizing anatomy yields an Euler
product under an explicit non-vanishing hypothesis on the ground-state
autocorrelation (Theorems~\ref{thm:convdet}--\ref{thm:char}), which holds on an
explicit range of lags when the ground state is close to the Gaussian. Where
$c_0(\log p^k)\ne0$, the anatomy determines the Satake power sums and hence the
local factors (Theorems~\ref{thm:satake} and~\ref{thm:gen}), and these behave as
usual under Rankin--Selberg products, isobaric sums and lifts
(\S\ref{sec:funct}). Open questions include an unconditional converse (whether
the non-vanishing hypothesis holds for the ground states of actual $Q_L$), and
whether factorizing anatomy together with the functional equation forces
automorphy in degree $\ge2$; in degree one the answer follows from~\cite{KP1}
(Theorem~\ref{thm:gl1}).
