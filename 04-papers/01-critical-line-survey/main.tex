\title[The Critical Line: A Survey]{The Critical Line: A Survey of Recent Progress on the Riemann Hypothesis}
\author{David Alejandro Trejo Pizzo}
\thanks{Independent Researcher. \texttt{dtrejopizzo@gmail.com}}

\begin{document}
\begin{abstract}
The Riemann Hypothesis (RH) asserts that all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function $\zeta(s)$ lie on the critical line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$. This survey describes recent work in three directions: the analytic approach, where Guth and Maynard gave a new large value estimate for Dirichlet polynomials, yielding the first improvement of the zero density estimates of Ingham and Huxley near $\sigma = 3/4$; the spectral approach, including Connes's absorption-spectrum picture, the prolate operators of Connes, Consani and Moscovici, and a reformulation of RH in the modular bootstrap; and the algebro-geometric approach, where the programme of Connes and Consani seeks a Riemann--Roch theorem and an intersection theory over the sphere spectrum $\mathbb{S}$ as an absolute base.

We give accounts of these developments, discuss how much arithmetic content the proposed geometric models carry, and describe the main obstacles. We also record that several reformulations proved equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis (the criteria of Weil and Li, the de Bruijn--Newman constant, the Nyman--Beurling approximation problem) are positivity or extremal statements, and that the geometric and conformal-field-theoretic programmes aim at positivity statements whose implication for the Riemann Hypothesis has not been established. The survey contains no new results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and historical context}\label{sec:intro}
%% ======================================================================

The Riemann zeta function, defined for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$ by the Dirichlet series
\[
\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^{-s} = \prod_{p \text{ prime}} (1 - p^{-s})^{-1},
\]
admits a meromorphic continuation to the entire complex plane with a simple pole at $s = 1$. The Euler product relates the analytic properties of $\zeta(s)$ to the distribution of prime numbers. The \emph{non-trivial zeros}, those in the critical strip $0 < \operatorname{Re}(s) < 1$, govern the error term in the Prime Number Theorem.

\begin{conjecture}[Riemann, 1859]\label{conj:rh}
All non-trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$ satisfy $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$.
\end{conjecture}

Riemann stated this in his 1859 memoir \cite{riemann1859}, noting that it was ``very probable'' (sehr wahrscheinlich) but that he had set it aside after a few brief unsuccessful attempts. The conjecture was included by Hilbert as the eighth of his 23 problems in 1900 and by the Clay Mathematics Institute as one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems in 2000 \cite{bombieri2000}.

\subsection{Consequences of the Riemann Hypothesis}\label{sec:consequences}

We recall some of the consequences of RH.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Prime distribution.} RH is equivalent to the estimate $\pi(x) = \operatorname{Li}(x) + O(\sqrt{x}\log x)$, where $\operatorname{Li}(x) = \int_2^x dt/\log t$. Unconditionally the best known error term is
\[
O\bigl(x\exp(-c(\log x)^{3/5}(\log\log x)^{-1/5})\bigr).
\]
\item \emph{Primes in short intervals.} RH implies that the interval $[x, x + C\sqrt{x}\log x]$ contains a prime for all sufficiently large $x$ and an appropriate constant $C$.
\item \emph{Arithmetic functions.} RH is equivalent to the bound $\sum_{n \le x} \mu(n) = O(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})$ for the summatory function of the M\"obius function.
\item \emph{The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis} (GRH) for Dirichlet $L$-functions has consequences for the distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions, the least quadratic non-residue, and primality testing (Miller's primality test is deterministic and polynomial-time under GRH).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What is known unconditionally}\label{sec:known}

The principal unconditional results concerning the zeros are the following.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Infinitely many zeros on the line.} Hardy \cite{hardy1914} proved that $\zeta(1/2 + it) = 0$ for infinitely many real $t$.
\item \emph{A positive proportion.} Selberg \cite{selberg1942} proved that a positive proportion of the zeros lie on the critical line, and explicit lower bounds for the proportion have been obtained since (Section~\ref{sec:proportion}).
\item \emph{Zero-free regions.} The zeta function has no zeros in explicit regions near $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ (Section~\ref{sec:zfr}).
\item \emph{Density estimates.} The number of zeros with real part at least $\sigma>1/2$ satisfies quantitative upper bounds (Section~\ref{sec:gm}).
\item \emph{Computational verification.} RH has been verified for the first $1.2 \times 10^{13}$ non-trivial zeros, that is, up to height $3 \times 10^{12}$ (Platt--Trudgian \cite{platt2021}; Section~\ref{sec:verification}).
\end{enumerate}
No known result excludes zeros off the critical line at heights beyond the range of computation.

\subsection{The function field analogue}\label{sec:ff-intro}

The Riemann Hypothesis is a theorem in the function field setting. For a smooth projective curve $C$ of genus $g$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$, the zeta function $Z(C/\mathbb{F}_q, T)$ is a rational function whose zeros all satisfy $|T| = q^{-1/2}$ (equivalently, the reciprocal roots of the numerator have absolute value $q^{1/2}$). This was proved by Weil \cite{weil1948} using the intersection theory of $C \times C$, and generalized by Deligne \cite{deligne1974} to smooth projective varieties of any dimension. The existence of a proof in the function field case is a guide for strategy: the geometric methods that work there might be adapted to $\mathbb{Z}$.

\subsection{Organization of this survey}

Section~\ref{sec:analytic} covers the analytic approach: zero-free regions, zero density estimates (including the estimate of Guth and Maynard), the proportion of zeros on the critical line, and extreme values. Section~\ref{sec:comp} discusses computational verification and the statistics of the zeros. Section~\ref{sec:spectral} treats the spectral approach: the Hilbert--P\'olya idea, the Berry--Keating Hamiltonian, Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture, Connes's trace formula, and the scalar modular bootstrap. Section~\ref{sec:geometric} covers the algebro-geometric approach through $\mathbb{F}_1$-geometry and the Connes--Consani programme. Section~\ref{sec:equiv} discusses reformulations equivalent to RH (the criteria of Li and Weil, the de Bruijn--Newman constant, the Nyman--Beurling criterion), Voronin universality and the role of the Euler product. Sections~\ref{sec:synthesis} and~\ref{sec:paths} contain a comparison of the approaches and a list of open problems; these two sections express the author's assessment.

For introductions to the Riemann Hypothesis we refer to the books of Edwards \cite{edwards1974} and Titchmarsh \cite{titchmarsh1986}, the survey of Conrey \cite{conrey2003}, and the accounts of Bombieri \cite{bombieri2000} and Sarnak \cite{sarnak2004}. For a recent essay on the problem, see Connes \cite{connes2026}.

\subsection{Zero-free regions}\label{sec:zfr}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

It is known unconditionally that $\zeta(s)$ has no zeros in a region of the form $\sigma \ge 1 - f(t)$ for an explicit function $f(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$.

The classical zero-free region, established by de la Vall\'ee Poussin \cite{delavallee1899} in 1899, three years after his proof of the Prime Number Theorem, takes the form $\sigma \ge 1 - c/\log|t|$ for an absolute constant $c > 0$. It is the form used in most explicit estimates in prime number theory.

For large $|t|$, Korobov \cite{korobov1958} and Vinogradov \cite{vinogradov1958} obtained in 1958 the region
\[
\sigma \ge 1 - \frac{c}{(\log|t|)^{2/3}(\log\log|t|)^{1/3}},
\]
which remains the best known asymptotically. The improvement over de la Vall\'ee Poussin comes from Vinogradov's method, which bounds the sums $\sum_{n\le N}n^{it}$ through mean values of Weyl sums. Progress since 1958 has consisted of improving the explicit constants.

The proof of a zero-free region rests on a positivity argument. The classical method uses the inequality
\[
3 + 4\cos\theta + \cos 2\theta = 2(1 + \cos\theta)^2 \ge 0
\]
to show that $\operatorname{Re}[3\log\zeta(\sigma) + 4\log\zeta(\sigma+it) + \log\zeta(\sigma+2it)] \ge 0$ for $\sigma > 1$, which forces $\zeta(\sigma + it) \neq 0$ in a region near $\sigma = 1$. Improvements come from more general non-negative trigonometric polynomials $\sum_{k=0}^{K} a_k \cos(k\theta)$ with optimized coefficients (subject to $a_k\ge0$ and $a_1>a_0$; the latter is what excludes a zero on the line $\sigma=1$).

Mossinghoff, Trudgian and Yang \cite{mossinghoff2024} improved the explicit constants, using trigonometric polynomials of higher degree together with the verification of RH up to height $3\times10^{12}$ \cite{platt2021}, which disposes of small $|t|$. Their results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:zfr}.

\begin{remark}
The exponent $2/3$ in the Korobov--Vinogradov region arises from bounds for the sums $\sum n^{it}$ obtained by Vinogradov's method. The main conjecture in Vinogradov's mean value theorem has since been proved by Bourgain--Demeter--Guth \cite{bourgain2016} and Wooley \cite{wooley2016} (see Section~\ref{sec:gm}), but this has not led to an improvement of the exponent in the zero-free region. To prove RH one would need the zero-free region $\sigma > 1/2$; the known regions have width tending to $0$ as $t \to \infty$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Guth--Maynard theorem on zero density}\label{sec:gm}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

One may ask instead how many zeros can lie off the critical line. This is the subject of zero density estimates. Let $N(\sigma, T)$ denote the number of zeros $\rho = \beta + i\gamma$ of $\zeta(s)$ with $\beta \ge \sigma$ and $|\gamma| \le T$. The Riemann Hypothesis asserts $N(\sigma, T) = 0$ for all $\sigma > 1/2$.

The connection between zero density and Dirichlet polynomials is classical: if $\zeta(s)$ has a zero at $\sigma + it$, then a related Dirichlet polynomial $D(t) = \sum_{n \sim N} b_n n^{it}$ takes a large value near $t$. Bounding $N(\sigma, T)$ therefore reduces to bounding the number of well-spaced points at which a Dirichlet polynomial is large.

\subsubsection{The bounds of Ingham and Huxley}

Ingham \cite{ingham1940} proved $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{3(1-\sigma)/(2-\sigma)}(\log T)^5$, and Huxley \cite{huxley1972} proved $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{3(1-\sigma)/(3\sigma-1)+\varepsilon}$ for $\sigma\ge 3/4$. Together they give $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{\frac{12}{5}(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}$ for $1/2\le\sigma\le1$, with equality of the two exponents at $\sigma=3/4$, where $N(3/4, T) \ll T^{3/5 + \varepsilon}$. Later work improved the estimates in other ranges of $\sigma$, but not near $\sigma = 3/4$, the range that determines the exponent in the prime number theorem for short intervals. Near $\sigma = 1$, explicit zero density estimates have been given by Bellotti \cite{bellotti2023}.

Ingham's bound rests on the mean value theorem for Dirichlet polynomials and Huxley's on the Montgomery--Hal\'asz large value estimate; at $\sigma=3/4$ both give $T^{3/5+o(1)}$, and no method improved this until 2024.

\subsubsection{The Guth--Maynard large value estimate}

Guth and Maynard \cite{guth2024} prove the following. Let $D(t)=\sum_{N\le n\le 2N}b_n n^{it}$ with $|b_n|\le 1$, and let $W\subset[0,T]$ be a $1$-separated set with $|D(t)|\ge V$ for $t\in W$. Then
\[
|W|\le T^{o(1)}\bigl(N^2V^{-2}+N^{18/5}V^{-4}+TN^{12/5}V^{-4}\bigr).
\]
For $V=N^{3/4}$ and $N=T^{4/5}$ this gives $|W|\le T^{13/25+o(1)}$, in place of the bound $T^{3/5+o(1)}$ given by both the mean value theorem and the Montgomery--Hal\'asz--Huxley estimate. The proof bounds $|W|$ through the largest singular value of the $|W|\times N$ matrix $M=(n^{it})_{t\in W,\,n\sim N}$: one expands $\operatorname{tr}((M^*M)^3)$, applies Poisson summation, and separates three contributions, the last of which is controlled by the additive energy of $W$ and by a theorem of Heath-Brown on Dirichlet polynomials evaluated on difference sets.

\subsubsection{Remark on the Vinogradov mean value theorem}

The Vinogradov mean value theorem concerns the mean value
\begin{equation}
\int_0^1 \cdots \int_0^1 \left| \sum_{n=1}^{N} e(n\alpha_1 + n^2 \alpha_2 + \cdots + n^k \alpha_k) \right|^{2s} \, d\alpha_1 \cdots d\alpha_k,
\end{equation}
where $e(x) = e^{2\pi i x}$. The main conjecture, now a theorem, asserts that this integral is $\ll N^{s+\varepsilon} + N^{2s - k(k+1)/2 + \varepsilon}$. It was proved for $k=3$ by Wooley \cite{wooley2016} by efficient congruencing and for all $k\ge 4$ by Bourgain, Demeter and Guth \cite{bourgain2016} by $\ell^2$-decoupling for the moment curve in $\mathbb{R}^k$. Its relevance to $\zeta$ is through bounds for $\sum n^{it}$ near the line $\sigma=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:zfr}). It is not an input of the Guth--Maynard theorem, whose proof does not use decoupling.

\subsubsection{The zero density estimate}

The large value estimate has the following consequence.

\begin{theorem}[Guth--Maynard \cite{guth2024}]\label{thm:gm}
For $1/2\le\sigma\le1$, $N(\sigma,T)\le T^{15(1-\sigma)/(3+5\sigma)+o(1)}$. Combined with Ingham's bound for $\sigma\le 7/10$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gm}
N(\sigma,T)\le T^{\frac{30}{13}(1-\sigma)+o(1)}\qquad(1/2\le\sigma\le 1).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The constant $30/13$ replaces the constant $12/5$ of Ingham and Huxley; the exponents of Ingham and of Guth--Maynard coincide at $\sigma=7/10$. At $\sigma=3/4$ the exponent drops from $3/5$ to $5/9$. The density hypothesis, $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{2(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}$, remains open.

\subsubsection{Consequences}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Primes in short intervals.} The estimate \eqref{eq:gm} gives the asymptotic formula $\pi(x+y)-\pi(x)\sim y/\log x$ for $y\ge x^{17/30+\varepsilon}$, improving the range $y\ge x^{7/12+\varepsilon}$ of Huxley \cite{huxley1972}, and for almost all $x\in[X,2X]$ when $y\ge X^{2/15+\varepsilon}$ \cite{guth2024}. (Existence of a prime in $[x,x+x^{0.525}]$ for large $x$ was already known by sieve methods \cite{bhp2001}.)

\item \emph{Density hypothesis.} Theorem~\ref{thm:gm} lowers the constant $A$ in $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{A(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}$ from $12/5$ to $30/13$; the density hypothesis is $A=2$.

\item \emph{Method.} The proof shows that large value estimates for Dirichlet polynomials of length $N$ at the level $V=N^{3/4}$ can be improved beyond what the mean value theorem and the Montgomery--Hal\'asz method give, by exploiting the additive structure of the set of large values.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Subsequent work}

Tao, Trudgian and Yang \cite{tao2025} introduced the \emph{Analytic Number Theory Exponent Database}, which records the known implications among exponent pairs, large value estimates, zero density exponents and additive energy exponents, and derives from them, with computer assistance, the best bounds that follow from the recorded inputs, the Guth--Maynard estimate among them.

Gafni and Tao \cite{gafni2025} study the number of exceptional intervals for the prime number theorem in short intervals, with zero density estimates as input: besides the range $\theta>17/30$ in which the asymptotic holds for all $x$, they bound the number of exceptional $x$ for smaller $\theta$. Hieu \cite{hieu2025} uses the bound \eqref{eq:gm} to obtain arithmetic progressions of primes in short intervals $[x,x+x^{\theta}]$ with $\theta > 17/30$.

\subsection{The proportion of zeros on the critical line}\label{sec:proportion}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

A complementary question is what proportion of the zeros can be proved to lie on the critical line.

Hardy \cite{hardy1914} proved that infinitely many zeros lie on the critical line, and Selberg \cite{selberg1942} proved that a positive proportion do. Levinson \cite{levinson1974} proved that more than $1/3$ of the zeros are on the critical line, and Conrey \cite{conrey1989} improved this to more than $2/5$.

The best proportion proved so far, due to Pratt, Robles, Zaharescu and Zeindler \cite{pratt2020}, is that more than $5/12$ of the non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line.

\subsubsection{The mollifier method}

These results use a \emph{mollifier}: a Dirichlet polynomial $M(s)$ designed to approximate $1/\zeta(s)$. The standard form is
\[
M(s) = \sum_{n \le y} \frac{\mu(n)}{n^s} P\!\left(\frac{\log(y/n)}{\log y}\right),
\]
where $\mu$ is the M\"obius function, $y = T^{\theta}$ for some $\theta > 0$, and $P$ is a polynomial with $P(0)=0$, $P(1)=1$, chosen to optimize the result \cite{conrey1989,levinson1974}.

Selberg's method detects sign changes of the real-valued function $Z(t)=e^{i\vartheta(t)}\zeta(\tfrac12+it)$, where $\vartheta(t)$ is the Riemann--Siegel theta function, using mollified mean values. Levinson's method, which underlies the results with explicit proportions, is different: the zeros of $\zeta$ off the critical line are related to the zeros of $G(s)=\zeta(s)+\zeta'(s)/\log T$ in $\operatorname{Re}s\ge1/2$, and the number of the latter is bounded, by Littlewood's lemma, by a mean value
\[
\int_0^T \left|G M\!\left(a+it\right)\right|^2 dt,\qquad a=\tfrac12-\frac{R}{\log T}.
\]
The proportion obtained depends on the length $\theta$ of the mollifier for which this mean value can be evaluated asymptotically.

\subsubsection{Recent methods}

The improvement to $5/12$ uses a more general mollifier, applied to a linear combination of $\zeta$ and its derivatives; the required twisted second moment is evaluated with techniques from the work on moments and ratios of Conrey, Farmer, Keating, Rubinstein and Snaith \cite{cfkrs2005}, of Conrey, Farmer and Zirnbauer \cite{conrey2008}, and of Conrey and Snaith \cite{conrey2007}. The ratios conjecture itself is not assumed; the result is unconditional.

The mollifier method is related to the theory of moments of $L$-functions. The conjecture of Keating and Snaith \cite{keating2000}, derived from random matrix theory, predicts
\[
\int_0^T \left|\zeta\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2k} dt \sim C_k T (\log T)^{k^2},
\]
with an explicit constant $C_k$ involving the Barnes $G$-function. It is known for $k=1,2$.

\subsubsection{The limits of the method}

The proportion obtained depends on the length $\theta$ of the mollifier for which the mollified second moment can be evaluated ($\theta<4/7$ for the classical mollifier, by Conrey \cite{conrey1989}). Even with arbitrarily long mollifiers Levinson's method is not known to give all zeros, and no proportion above $5/12$ has been proved.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Extreme values: resonance methods and log-correlated fields}\label{sec:resonance}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Another analytic direction concerns the maximal size of $\zeta(s)$ on the critical line.

\subsubsection{The resonance method}

Soundararajan \cite{soundararajan2008} introduced the \emph{resonance method}, which produces large values of $|\zeta(1/2 + it)|$ by comparing two mean values weighted by a ``resonator'' Dirichlet polynomial. This method yields
\[
\max_{T\le t\le 2T}|\zeta(\tfrac12+it)|\ge\exp\Bigl((1+o(1))\sqrt{\tfrac{\log T}{\log\log T}}\Bigr).
\]
Bondarenko and Seip \cite{bondarenko2017}, using a resonator built from greatest-common-divisor sums, obtained the larger lower bound
\[
\max_{0\le t\le T}|\zeta(\tfrac12+it)|\ge\exp\Bigl(\bigl(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}+o(1)\bigr)\sqrt{\tfrac{\log T\,\log\log\log T}{\log\log T}}\Bigr).
\]

In the other direction, RH implies $|\zeta(\tfrac12+it)|\le\exp\bigl(C\log t/\log\log t\bigr)$ for large $t$ (Littlewood); the true order of the maximum is not known.

\subsubsection{Log-correlated fields and multiplicative chaos}

For $\tau$ chosen at random in $[T,2T]$, the function $h\mapsto\log|\zeta(1/2 + i\tau+ih)|$, $|h|\le 1$, behaves in several respects like a \emph{log-correlated Gaussian field}, the structure that appears in the theory of Gaussian free fields and multiplicative chaos. The conjecture of Fyodorov, Hiary and Keating \cite{fyodorov2012} predicts that, for all $\tau\in[T,2T]$ outside a set of measure $o(T)$,
\[
\max_{|h|\le1}\log|\zeta(\tfrac12+i\tau+ih)|=\log\log T-\tfrac34\log\log\log T+O(1),
\]
the $O(1)$ term being bounded in probability. The leading term was established by Najnudel \cite{najnudel2018} (conditionally on RH) and by Arguin, Belius, Bourgade, Radziwi\l\l{} and Soundararajan \cite{abbrs2019} unconditionally; Arguin, Bourgade and Radziwi\l\l{} \cite{abr1,abr2} have since obtained the second-order term, with matching upper and lower bounds for the $O(1)$ term.

The theory of Gaussian multiplicative chaos was initiated by Kahane \cite{kahane1985}; see the review of Rhodes and Vargas \cite{rhodes2014}. Saksman and Webb \cite{saksman2020} proved that the randomly shifted function $x\mapsto\zeta(1/2+ix+i\omega T)$, with $\omega$ uniform in $[0,1]$, converges in law as $T\to\infty$ to a random generalized function, which can be written as the product of a Gaussian multiplicative chaos distribution and a smooth random function.

These results describe the statistical behaviour of $\zeta$ on the critical line; they do not bear directly on RH.

\section{Computation and statistics of zeros}\label{sec:comp}
%% ======================================================================

Computation plays two roles in the study of the Riemann Hypothesis: the verification of RH for finitely many zeros, and the statistical study of the zeros.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Rigorous verification}\label{sec:verification}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

One verifies that all zeros up to a given height lie on the critical line by Turing's method \cite{turing1953}, which combines the argument principle with the sign changes of the Hardy function $Z(t) = e^{i\vartheta(t)}\zeta(1/2+it)$, where $\vartheta$ is the Riemann--Siegel theta function.

Platt and Trudgian \cite{platt2021} verify RH for all zeros with $0<\gamma\le H=3\,000\,175\,332\,800$, that is, for the first
\[
N(H) \approx 1.236 \times 10^{13} \quad \text{non-trivial zeros.}
\]
The two quantities are related by the Riemann--von Mangoldt formula:
\[
N(T) = \frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi e} + O(\log T).
\]

The method works as follows. The number of zeros with imaginary part in $(0, T]$ equals $N(T) = \frac{1}{\pi}\vartheta(T) + 1 + S(T)$, where $S(T) = \frac{1}{\pi}\arg\zeta(1/2 + iT)$. The function $Z(t)$ is real for real $t$, and each of its sign changes gives a zero on the critical line. Turing's method uses a bound for $\int_{t_1}^{t_2}S(t)\,dt$ to determine $N(T)$ exactly from values of $Z$ near $T$; if the number of sign changes found equals $N(T)$, all zeros up to height $T$ are simple and lie on the critical line.

The explicit zero-free regions of Section~\ref{sec:zfr} use this computation as an input: their constants depend on the height up to which RH has been verified.

\begin{remark}
The Platt--Trudgian result is a theorem: the computation uses interval arithmetic throughout. No finite verification can prove RH. Its uses are (a) as an input for explicit analytic estimates, (b) as a test of the hypothesis at low heights, and (c) as a source of statistical data about the zeros.
\end{remark}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Odlyzko computations and GUE statistics}\label{sec:odlyzko}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

To study the statistics of the zeros one examines zeros at much greater heights. Odlyzko computed large blocks of zeros at great heights, up to the neighbourhood of the $10^{23}$-rd zero (height about $1.3\times10^{22}$); see \cite{odlyzko1987,hiary2012}.

At these heights the statistics of the normalized zeros agree closely with those of the eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices, the \emph{Gaussian Unitary Ensemble} (GUE). In particular, the pair correlation function of the normalized zeros matches the GUE density
\[
1 - \left(\frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi x}\right)^2.
\]

This agreement is the main empirical support for the idea that the zeros behave statistically like the eigenvalues of a random Hermitian matrix. A Poisson process would show no repulsion between nearby points.

The numerical studies of Odlyzko \cite{odlyzko1987} first exhibited this agreement, and later computations at greater heights confirmed it. The agreement extends beyond pair correlation: the nearest-neighbour spacing distribution, the number variance, and the $n$-level correlation functions all match the GUE predictions within the accuracy of the data.

The convergence to the GUE statistics is slow: at height $10^{22}$ deviations from the limit are visible, and they have arithmetic structure. Bogomolny and Keating \cite{bogomolny1996}, and later Conrey and Snaith \cite{conrey2007} starting from the ratios conjecture, gave a conjectural formula for the pair correlation at height $T$ with all lower-order terms: for suitable test functions $f$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bk}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{0<\gamma,\gamma'\le T}f(\gamma-\gamma')={}&\frac1{(2\pi)^2}\int_0^T\biggl(2\pi f(0)\log\frac t{2\pi}+\int_{-T}^{T}f(r)\Bigl[\log^2\frac t{2\pi}+2\Bigl(\Bigl(\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}\Bigr)'(1+ir)\\
&+\Bigl(\frac t{2\pi}\Bigr)^{-ir}\zeta(1-ir)\zeta(1+ir)A(ir)-B(ir)\Bigr)\Bigr]dr\biggr)dt+O(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the inner integral is a principal value at $r=0$ and
\[
A(\eta)=\prod_p\frac{(1-p^{-1-\eta})(1-2p^{-1}+p^{-1-\eta})}{(1-p^{-1})^2},\qquad B(\eta)=\sum_p\Bigl(\frac{\log p}{p^{1+\eta}-1}\Bigr)^2 .
\]
The leading term gives the GUE density; the remaining terms involve $\zeta$ on the line $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ and account for the deviations seen in the data.

Thus the zeros follow random matrix statistics in the limit, while at finite height their correlations retain information about the primes.

\section{The spectral approach: the search for an operator}\label{sec:spectral}
%% ======================================================================

The spectral approach seeks to realize the non-trivial zeros as eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space. If there were a self-adjoint operator $H$ whose eigenvalues $E_n$ satisfied $\rho_n = 1/2 + iE_n$, with all zeros accounted for, then $E_n \in \mathbb{R}$ would give $\operatorname{Re}(\rho_n) = 1/2$.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Hilbert--P\'olya idea: history and attempts}\label{sec:hp}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

The idea that the zeros of $\zeta(s)$ have a spectral origin is attributed to Hilbert and P\'olya. P\'olya described it in a 1982 letter to Odlyzko as a suggestion he made to Landau around 1914: the reality of the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator would explain the critical line. No written record from Hilbert survives.

The idea gained support from three later developments: (a) Selberg's trace formula (1956), in which the zeros of the Selberg zeta function of a compact Riemann surface are given by the eigenvalues of the Laplacian; (b) Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture (1973, Section~\ref{sec:montgomery}), according to which the zeros have the same local statistics as eigenvalues of random Hermitian matrices; and (c) the Berry--Keating proposal (1999, Section~\ref{sec:bk}) of a classical Hamiltonian whose quantization should produce the zeros.

\subsubsection{The de Branges approach}

Beginning in the 1980s, de Branges proposed to use the theory of Hilbert spaces of entire functions to prove RH. His approach consists in showing that certain positivity conditions hold for functionals associated to $\zeta(s)$.

This programme has not produced a proof of RH. The situation is as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The positivity conditions proposed would imply RH, so they are at least as hard to establish as RH itself.
\item Conrey and Li \cite{conreyli2000} showed that the positivity conditions of the version of the argument they examined are not satisfied in the case of $\zeta$.
\item The framework has not yielded partial results (such as zero density estimates or proportion theorems) that would test the approach independently of the final claim.
\end{enumerate}

This illustrates a recurring difficulty: many reformulations of RH are equivalent to it without being easier. A spectral reformulation is useful only if it connects RH with structures in which independent methods of proof are available.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Berry--Keating Hamiltonian}\label{sec:bk}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Berry and Keating \cite{berry1999} proposed that the classical Hamiltonian
\[
H_{\mathrm{cl}} = xp
\]
(where $x$ is position and $p$ is momentum) is related to the Riemann zeros.

\subsubsection{Semiclassical evidence}

The classical trajectories are the hyperbolas $x(t) = x_0 e^t$, $p(t) = p_0 e^{-t}$. Counting Planck cells in the region $x\ge\ell_x$, $p\ge\ell_p$, $xp\le E$ with $\ell_x\ell_p=2\pi\hbar$ (and $\hbar=1$) gives
\[
\bar{N}(E) = \frac{E}{2\pi}\log\frac{E}{2\pi e}+O(1),
\]
which agrees with the main term of the Riemann--von Mangoldt formula $N(T) = \frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi e} + O(\log T)$. The agreement depends on the choice of the cut-offs.

\subsubsection{The quantization problem}

The quantization $\hat{H} = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{x}\hat{p} + \hat{p}\hat{x})$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ has continuous spectrum. To obtain a discrete spectrum one must impose boundary conditions or restrict the phase space.

The generalized eigenfunctions of $\hat{H}$ on $L^2(0,\infty)$ are $\psi_E(x) = x^{-1/2 + iE}$, which are not square-integrable. On $L^2(0,\infty)$ the operator is essentially self-adjoint with purely continuous spectrum. To produce a discrete spectrum matching the zeros, several proposals have been made:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Restricting to a compact region of phase space. This requires a choice of boundary conditions tied to the primes, and no such choice is known that does not presuppose the zeros.
\item Adding a confining potential $V(x)$ that preserves the asymptotic density of states. The operator $\hat{H} = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{x}\hat{p} + \hat{p}\hat{x}) + V(\hat{x})$ then has discrete spectrum, but no choice of $V$ has been shown to yield the zeros.
\item Working on Connes's ad\`ele class space (Section~\ref{sec:connes-spectral}), where the role of the boundary conditions is played by the $p$-adic completions of $\mathbb{Q}$.
\end{enumerate}
None of these has been carried through.

\subsubsection{Other spectral models}

LeClair and Mussardo \cite{leclair2024} proposed a model of a particle scattering off impurities on a circle whose Bethe-ansatz quantization condition has as solutions the ordinates of the zeros on the critical line. Both the functional equation and the Euler product enter the construction. For the Davenport--Heilbronn function, which satisfies a functional equation but has no Euler product (Section~\ref{sec:whyhard}), the same equations also have pairs of solutions off the line. This is not a proof of RH; it is a model in which the difference between $\zeta$ and a function without Euler product is visible.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Montgomery pair correlation conjecture}\label{sec:montgomery}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Montgomery \cite{montgomery1973} studied the pair correlation of the zeros, that is, the distribution of the differences between zeros on the scale of the mean spacing. He proved, under RH, that for test functions $f$ with $\operatorname{supp}\hat f\subset(-1,1)$ the pair correlation is given by the density
\[
1 - \left(\frac{\sin \pi u}{\pi u}\right)^2,
\]
which is the pair correlation function of the eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices (GUE), as Dyson pointed out to Montgomery at the Institute for Advanced Study.

\begin{conjecture}[Montgomery, 1973]\label{conj:montgomery}
For every Schwartz function $f$,
\[
\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{N(T)} \sum_{\substack{0 < \gamma, \gamma' \le T \\ \gamma \neq \gamma'}} f\!\left(\frac{(\gamma - \gamma')\log T}{2\pi}\right) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(u)\left(1 - \left(\frac{\sin \pi u}{\pi u}\right)^2\right) du.
\]
\end{conjecture}

Extending Montgomery's theorem to test functions whose Fourier transform has larger support is open; it is related to the distribution of primes in short intervals and to prime pairs.

\subsubsection{Higher correlations and the ratios conjecture}

The $n$-level correlations were studied by Rudnick and Sarnak \cite{rudnick1996}, who proved, for test functions with restricted Fourier support, that the $n$-level correlations of the zeros of cuspidal automorphic $L$-functions on $\mathrm{GL}_m$ agree with those of GUE (unconditionally for $m\le3$, and under a hypothesis on the Satake parameters in general). The GUE statistics are therefore not specific to $\zeta(s)$.

The \emph{ratios conjecture} of Conrey, Farmer and Zirnbauer \cite{conrey2008} predicts averages of ratios of $L$-functions, from which the correlation functions of the zeros can be derived, lower-order terms included. Its predictions agree with the cases that can be proved, where the test functions have restricted support.

\subsubsection{Significance}

\begin{itemize}
\item The pair correlation conjecture and its generalizations are the main quantitative evidence that the zeros behave like the eigenvalues of a random matrix.
\item The computations of Odlyzko (Section~\ref{sec:odlyzko}) agree with the GUE predictions far beyond the range of test functions covered by Montgomery's theorem.
\item Random matrix theory has produced, through the work of Keating and Snaith \cite{keating2000}, conjectures for moments of $L$-functions that have guided work on mollifiers and moments; see the survey of Conrey \cite{conrey2003}.
\item Since GUE statistics occur for many families of $L$-functions, a proof of RH from the spectral direction would be expected to apply to a broad class of $L$-functions.
\end{itemize}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Connes's absorption spectrum and prolate wave operators}\label{sec:connes-spectral}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

In the Berry--Keating picture the zeros appear as an ``emission spectrum'', the eigenvalues of a Hamiltonian. Connes \cite{connes1999} proposed a spectral realization in which the zeros appear as an \emph{absorption spectrum}: values that are missing from a continuous spectrum, like dark lines in the spectrum of a star.

\subsubsection{The ad\`ele class space and the trace formula}

Connes considers the space of ad\`ele classes $\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times}$ with the scaling action of the id\`ele class group. This space involves all completions of $\mathbb{Q}$, the $p$-adic fields $\mathbb{Q}_p$ and $\mathbb{R}$. Connes \cite{connes1999} obtained a spectral realization of the zeros on the critical line as an absorption spectrum, proved a trace formula of this type for every finite set of places (the semilocal case), and showed that the corresponding global trace formula on the ad\`ele class space is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis for all $L$-functions with Gr\"ossencharakter; the geometric side of that formula is Weil's explicit formula.

\subsubsection{Weil positivity and time-frequency localization}

For $g\in C_c^\infty(0,\infty)$ let $\widehat g(s)=\int_0^\infty g(x)x^{s-1}dx$, $\tilde g(x)=x^{-1}\overline{g(1/x)}$, $(g*h)(x)=\int_0^\infty g(x/y)h(y)\,dy/y$, and
\[
W(g)=\sum_\rho\widehat g(\rho),
\]
the sum over the non-trivial zeros with multiplicity; by the explicit formula, $W(g)$ is also a sum over the prime powers plus archimedean and polar terms. Then $W(g*\tilde g)=\sum_\rho\widehat g(\rho)\overline{\widehat g(1-\bar\rho)}$, and Weil's criterion \cite{weil1952} states that RH holds if and only if $W(g*\tilde g)\ge0$ for all such $g$. We refer to this condition as \emph{Weil positivity}. The difficulty lies in controlling the interaction between the archimedean and the non-archimedean contributions.

In classical harmonic analysis (Slepian, Landau, Pollak \cite{slepian1961}) one studies the compact operator $P_\Lambda B_\Omega P_\Lambda$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$,
\[
(P_\Lambda B_\Omega P_\Lambda f)(x)=\mathbf 1_{[-\Lambda,\Lambda]}(x)\int_{-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\frac{\sin\Omega(x-y)}{\pi(x-y)}f(y)\,dy,
\]
where $P_\Lambda$ restricts to $[-\Lambda,\Lambda]$ and $B_\Omega$ restricts the Fourier transform to $[-\Omega,\Omega]$. Its eigenfunctions are the prolate spheroidal wave functions; the first of them maximizes the fraction of energy in $[-\Lambda,\Lambda]$ among functions band-limited to $[-\Omega,\Omega]$. This operator commutes with the \emph{prolate wave operator}
\[
W_\lambda=-\partial_x\bigl((\lambda^2-x^2)\partial_x\bigr)+(2\pi\lambda x)^2
\]
(with $\Lambda=\lambda$, $\Omega=2\pi\lambda$), a second-order differential operator.

Connes, Consani and Moscovici \cite{connes2024b} study the self-adjoint extension of $W_\lambda$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and its semilocal analogues, and relate them to Sonin spaces and to the semilocal trace formula. In \cite{connes2025} the same authors introduce \emph{zeta spectral triples}, which place these operators in the framework of noncommutative geometry. These results concern finitely many places. Weil positivity in the global sense required for RH has not been established: the passage from the semilocal results to a statement involving the full Euler product is open.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The modular bootstrap}\label{sec:bootstrap}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

A connection between the Riemann zeta function and conformal field theory (CFT) was found by Benjamin and Chang \cite{benjamin2022}. For two-dimensional CFTs with $U(1)^c$ symmetry, they derived a crossing equation for the scalar primary operators in which the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta$ appear.

Here consistency conditions of quantum field theory are used to obtain information involving $\zeta(s)$, and RH is rephrased as a statement about the density of scalar operators.

\subsubsection{Modular invariance and the crossing equation}

The torus partition function $Z(\tau)$ of a CFT in two dimensions, with $\tau$ the modular parameter, is invariant under the modular group; in particular
\begin{equation}
Z(-1/\tau) = Z(\tau).
\end{equation}
Using harmonic analysis on the modular fundamental domain, Benjamin and Chang derive from this a crossing equation involving only the scalar primary operators; the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta$ enter through the spectral decomposition, and RH can be rephrased as a statement about the asymptotic density of scalar operators \cite{benjamin2022}. The equation yields bounds on the scalar gap.

\subsubsection{Unitarity}

Unitarity requires the Hilbert space of the CFT to have a positive-definite inner product, so that the degeneracies $d(\Delta)$ of the operators of dimension $\Delta$ are non-negative integers. Such positivity is what makes bootstrap bounds possible. No constraint on the zeros of $\zeta$ has been derived from unitarity; any analogy between unitarity and Weil positivity is, at present, an analogy only.

\begin{remark}
The result of Benjamin and Chang is a reformulation of RH in the language of the modular bootstrap: the non-trivial zeros enter a scalar crossing equation, and RH becomes a statement about the asymptotic density of scalar operators in certain two-dimensional CFTs. It is not an argument that unitarity implies RH: no contradiction has been derived from a hypothetical zero off the line. The reformulation makes the tools of the conformal bootstrap, such as semidefinite programming, available for questions involving $\zeta$.
\end{remark}

\section{The algebro-geometric approach: from function fields to \texorpdfstring{$\mathbb{F}_1$}{F1}}\label{sec:geometric}
%% ======================================================================

The analogue of RH is a theorem for the zeta function of a smooth projective curve $C$ over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$: it was proved by Weil \cite{weil1948}, and Deligne \cite{deligne1974} proved the corresponding statement for smooth projective varieties (the last of the Weil conjectures). The geometric strategy for RH over $\mathbb{Q}$ seeks a geometry for $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ that parallels the geometry of curves over finite fields closely enough to admit an analogous proof.

For an overview of the function field case and its relation to RH, see Bombieri \cite{bombieri2000}.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The function field template}\label{sec:ff}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Weil's proof of RH for function fields uses three ingredients:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{The surface.} The product $C \times_{\mathbb{F}_q} C$ is a smooth projective surface over $\mathbb{F}_q$ with an intersection theory.
\item \emph{Riemann--Roch.} The Riemann--Roch theorem for the surface relates the dimensions of spaces of sections of line bundles to intersection numbers.
\item \emph{The Hodge index theorem.} The intersection form on the N\'eron--Severi group of $C \times_{\mathbb{F}_q} C$ has signature $(1, \rho-1)$, where $\rho$ is the rank of the N\'eron--Severi group. A consequence is the Castelnuovo--Severi inequality $D^2\le 2d_1d_2$ for a divisor $D$ with degrees $d_1=D\cdot(\{P\}\times C)$ and $d_2=D\cdot(C\times\{P\})$. Applied to combinations of the diagonal $\Delta$ and the graphs of the powers of Frobenius, it yields $|\alpha_i| = q^{1/2}$ for the reciprocal roots $\alpha_i$ of the numerator of the zeta function, which is the Riemann Hypothesis for $C$.
\end{enumerate}

The zeta function of $C/\mathbb{F}_q$ has the form $Z(C/\mathbb{F}_q, T) = P(T)/[(1-T)(1-qT)]$, where $P(T) = \prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1 - \alpha_i T)$. The number $N_n=\#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^n})$ of fixed points of $\operatorname{Fr}^n$ is the intersection number of the graph $\Gamma_n$ of $\operatorname{Fr}^n$ with the diagonal $\Delta$ in $C \times_{\mathbb{F}_q} C$. For integers $a,b$ and $n\ge1$ let $D=a\Delta+b\,\Gamma_n$. Its degrees are $d_1=a+b$ and $d_2=a+bq^n$, and $\Delta^2=2-2g$, $\Gamma_n^2=q^n(2-2g)$, $\Delta\cdot\Gamma_n=N_n$. The inequality $D^2\le 2d_1d_2$ reads
\[
-2g\,a^2+2ab\,(N_n-1-q^n)-2g\,q^n b^2\le0\qquad\text{for all }a,b,
\]
hence $|N_n-1-q^n|\le 2g\,q^{n/2}$. Since $N_n=1+q^n-\sum_i\alpha_i^n$, this gives $|\sum_i\alpha_i^n|\le 2gq^{n/2}$ for all $n$, so $|\alpha_i|\le q^{1/2}$, and the functional equation, under which $\alpha\mapsto q/\alpha$ permutes the $\alpha_i$, gives $|\alpha_i|=q^{1/2}$.

\subsubsection{The obstacle for \texorpdfstring{$\mathbb{Z}$}{Z}}

To repeat this for $\mathbb{Z}$ one faces an obstruction: $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ is the terminal object in the category of schemes. There is no base field beneath it over which it can be viewed as a curve. One therefore postulates a geometry over a ``field with one element'' $\mathbb{F}_1$. The problem is to construct a geometry that supports algebraic cycles, an intersection theory, and a positivity property playing the role of the Hodge index theorem.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Connes--Consani programme and the sphere spectrum}\label{sec:cc}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Connes and Consani have pursued the $\mathbb{F}_1$-strategy with tools from noncommutative geometry, topos theory and homotopy theory. An account of the BC-system, absolute cyclotomy and the quantized calculus is given in \cite{connes2022}.

\subsubsection{From the scaling site to \texorpdfstring{$\mathbb{S}$}{S}}

An earlier stage of the programme is based on the \emph{scaling site} \cite{connes2016}, the topos $[0,\infty)\rtimes\mathbb{N}^\times$ with a structure sheaf of tropical type, obtained from the arithmetic site (built on the multiplicative monoid $\mathbb{N}^\times$) by extension of scalars. In this setting the action of $\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}$ plays the role that the Frobenius plays for curves over finite fields.

More recently the absolute base has been taken to be the sphere spectrum $\mathbb{S}$, treated through Segal's $\Gamma$-sets. In stable homotopy theory $\mathbb{S}$ is the initial ring spectrum, and in this formulation it plays the role of $\mathbb{F}_1$. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is then an algebra over the base $\mathbb{S}$, with $\mathbb{Z}=\pi_0(\mathbb{S})$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=large, column sep=huge]

\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}) \times_{\mathbb{S}} \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}) & \Lambda_{\text{scaling}} \arrow[l, hook']
\end{tikzcd}
\caption{The analogy between the graph of Frobenius on $C\times_{\mathbb{F}_q}C$ and the scaling flow $\Lambda_{\text{scaling}}$ on a hypothetical arithmetic surface over the sphere spectrum $\mathbb{S}$. The lower row is not a constructed object.}
\label{fig:frobenius_analogy}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Riemann--Roch for \texorpdfstring{$\mathbb{Z}$}{Z}}

Connes and Consani \cite{connes2024} proved a Riemann--Roch formula for $\overline{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}}$ over the base $\mathbb{S}$: for an Arakelov divisor $D$ the integer-valued Euler characteristic, defined through $\mathbb{S}$-modules $H^0(D)$ and $H^1(D)$, equals $\lceil\deg_2D\rceil'+1$, where $\deg_2=\deg/\log 2$ is the degree computed with logarithms to base $2$ and $\lceil x\rceil'$ is the right-continuous function that agrees with $\lceil x\rceil$ for non-integer $x>0$ and with $-\lceil -x\rceil$ for non-integer $x<0$.

This is a theorem. However, a Riemann--Roch formula alone does not constrain the zeros: in Weil's proof the formula is used on the surface $C\times C$, together with the intersection pairing. It is not known whether the formula of \cite{connes2024} gives information on the location of the zeros of $\zeta(s)$ beyond what follows from the functional equation.

\subsubsection{The open step: algebraic cycles on the square}

The remaining ingredient of Weil's strategy, an intersection theory on the product and a Hodge index theorem, is open.

The difficulty is not only the construction of the product $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}) \times_{\mathbb{S}} \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$, but the definition of algebraic cycles on it. To carry out Weil's argument one must intersect the diagonal $\Delta$ with the graph of the scaling action. Without a theory of cycles and an intersection pairing with numerical values, the inequality $D^2 \le 2d_1d_2$ cannot be stated.

\subsubsection{Assessment of the geometric strategy}\label{sec:cc-critique}

The following obstacles stand between this programme and a proof of RH.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{The absence of Hodge theory.} The Hodge index theorem is proved by means of Hodge theory or of the Riemann--Roch theorem for surfaces. No analogue is available in the tropical or homotopy-theoretic settings, so the signature of a hypothetical intersection form is unknown.
\item \emph{Arithmetic content.} A proof of RH must use properties specific to $\zeta(s)$. It is not clear how a geometry over $\mathbb{S}$ would distinguish $\zeta(s)$ from linear combinations of $L$-functions that satisfy a functional equation and for which the analogue of RH is false (Section~\ref{sec:whyhard}).
\item \emph{The missing surface.} Arakelov geometry compactifies $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ by an archimedean place and has an intersection theory with a Hodge index theorem for arithmetic surfaces (Faltings \cite{faltings1984}, Hriljac \cite{hriljac1985}). These are surfaces fibred over $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$; the object needed for Weil's argument is a product of $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ with itself over a smaller base, which does not exist for schemes because $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ is terminal. Whether a base such as $\mathbb{S}$ supplies a product with an intersection pairing of the required signature is open.
\end{enumerate}

%% ======================================================================
\section{Reformulations and equivalent conditions}\label{sec:equiv}
%% ======================================================================

We describe several statements equivalent to RH, and two general features of $\zeta$ that any proof must take into account.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Li's criterion}\label{sec:li}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Li's criterion \cite{li1997} states that the Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to $\lambda_n\ge0$ for all $n\ge1$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li}
\lambda_n = \sum_{\rho} \left[ 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{\rho}\right)^{\!n} \right],
\end{equation}
where the sum is over the non-trivial zeros $\rho$ of $\zeta(s)$, counted with multiplicity, and is taken as the limit as $T\to\infty$ of the sums over $|\operatorname{Im}\rho|\le T$.

\subsubsection{Size and computation}

Under RH, $\lambda_n=\tfrac n2\log n+O(n)$. If RH fails, there is a zero $\rho$ with $|1-1/\rho|>1$, and its contribution to \eqref{eq:li} grows exponentially in modulus. The numbers $\lambda_n$ have been computed for $n\le 10^5$ and are positive in that range \cite{johansson2015}.

\subsubsection{Generalizations and connections}

Li's criterion extends to other $L$-functions; for automorphic $L$-functions see Lagarias \cite{lagarias2007}.

Bombieri and Lagarias \cite{bombieri1999} showed that Li's criterion is a special case of Weil's (Section~\ref{sec:connes-spectral}). Let $g_n(x)=P_n(\log x)$ for $0<x<1$, $g_n(1)=n/2$, $g_n(x)=0$ for $x>1$, where $P_n(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom nj\frac{x^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}$. Its Mellin transform is $\widehat g_n(s)=1-(1-1/s)^n$, so $\lambda_n=W(g_n)$, the functional $W$ being extended to these test functions, and $W(g_n*\tilde g_n)=2\lambda_n$. Thus the inequalities $\lambda_n\ge0$ are a countable subfamily of the Weil inequalities $W(g*\tilde g)\ge 0$.

The same paper gives the arithmetic expression
\[
\lambda_n=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom nj\eta_{j-1}+1-\frac n2(\gamma+\log4\pi)+\sum_{j=2}^{n}(-1)^j\binom nj(1-2^{-j})\zeta(j),
\]
where $\gamma$ is Euler's constant and $-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(s+1)=\frac1s+\sum_{j\ge0}\eta_js^j$; the first sum is the contribution of the primes and the remaining terms are the archimedean and polar contributions.

\subsubsection{Assessment}

Li's criterion is an equivalent reformulation of RH, and proving $\lambda_n \ge 0$ for all $n$ is as hard as proving RH. Its interest lies in its relation to Weil's criterion. The numerical positivity for $n \le 10^5$ is consistent with RH; it gives no information about larger $n$.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The de Bruijn--Newman constant}\label{sec:dbn}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Another reformulation expresses RH through a one-parameter deformation of the zeta function under the backward heat equation. Consider the entire function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ht}
H_t(z) = \int_0^\infty e^{t u^2}\, \Phi(u)\, \cos(z u)\, du,
\qquad
\Phi(u) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left( 2\pi^2 n^4 e^{9u} - 3\pi n^2 e^{5u} \right) e^{-\pi n^2 e^{4u}},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is positive, even and decays doubly exponentially. One has $H_0(z)=\tfrac18\,\xi\bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac{iz}{2}\bigr)$ with $\xi(s)=\tfrac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)$, so the zeros of $H_0$ are the numbers $z=2\gamma$, where $\tfrac12+i\gamma$ runs over the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta$; thus RH is equivalent to the assertion that all zeros of $H_0$ are real.

De Bruijn \cite{debruijn1950} proved that $H_t$ has only real zeros for $t\ge1/2$, and that if $H_t$ has only real zeros then so does $H_{t'}$ for every $t'>t$. Newman \cite{newman1976} proved that there is a finite constant $\Lambda\le1/2$, the \emph{de Bruijn--Newman constant}, such that $H_t$ has only real zeros if and only if $t \ge \Lambda$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\text{RH} \iff \Lambda \le 0.
\end{equation}
Newman conjectured that $\Lambda \ge 0$, and Rodgers and Tao \cite{rodgers2020} proved it. Combining the two,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-zero}
\boxed{\;\text{RH} \iff \Lambda = 0.\;}
\end{equation}
Thus, if RH holds, $H_t$ has only real zeros for every $t\ge0$ and for no $t<0$. The proof of Rodgers and Tao is by contradiction: if $\Lambda<0$, the dynamics of the zeros of $H_t$ would force the zeros of $H_0$ to be locally almost equally spaced, which is incompatible with known results on the gaps between zeros of $\zeta$. On the upper side, the Polymath~15 project proved $\Lambda\le0.22$ \cite{polymath2019}, and Platt and Trudgian \cite{platt2021} improved this to $\Lambda\le 0.2$, so that $0\le\Lambda\le0.2$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda-zero} states RH in terms of a flow. It is a statement about the threshold of real-rootedness in a one-parameter family, not a positivity condition in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:synthesis}.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Nyman--Beurling--B\'aez-Duarte criterion}\label{sec:nb}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

A third equivalence states RH as an approximation problem in a Hilbert space. Write $\{x\}$ for the fractional part and, for $0<\theta\le1$, let $\rho_\theta(x)=\{\theta/x\}-\theta\{1/x\}$, a function supported in $(0,1]$. The Nyman--Beurling criterion (Nyman \cite{nyman1950}; Beurling \cite{beurling1955}) states:
\begin{equation}
\text{RH} \iff \mathbf{1}_{(0,1)} \in \overline{\operatorname{span}}_{\,L^2(0,1)} \{\rho_\theta : 0 < \theta \le 1\}.
\end{equation}

B\'aez-Duarte \cite{baezduarte2003} showed that integer dilations suffice. Let $e_k(x)=\{1/(kx)\}$, an element of $L^2(0,\infty)$, and
\begin{equation}
d_N^2 = \inf_{c_1,\dots,c_N} \Bigl\| \mathbf{1}_{(0,1]} - \sum_{k=1}^N c_k\, e_k \Bigr\|_{L^2(0,\infty)}^2 .
\end{equation}
Then RH holds if and only if $d_N \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$. B\'aez-Duarte, Balazard, Landreau and Saias \cite{bdbls2000} proved that $\liminf_{N\to\infty} d_N^2\log N\ge\sum_{\operatorname{Re}\rho=1/2}|\rho|^{-2}$, the sum being over the distinct zeros $\rho$ of $\zeta$ on the critical line, and conjectured that
\begin{equation}
d_N^2 \sim \frac{C}{\log N}, \qquad C = 2+\gamma-\log 4\pi=0.0461914\ldots;
\end{equation}
under RH the constant $C$ equals $\sum_{\rho}|\rho|^{-2}$, the sum over all non-trivial zeros with multiplicity. The quantity $d_N$ is the solution of a finite least-squares problem whose Gram matrix $\langle e_j,e_k\rangle$ is explicit (Vasyunin's formula \cite{vasyunin1995}). As with Li's criterion, proving $d_N\to0$ is as hard as RH.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Voronin universality}\label{sec:universality}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Voronin \cite{voronin1975} proved that $\zeta(s)$ is \emph{universal}: non-vanishing holomorphic functions on compact subsets of the strip $1/2 < \operatorname{Re}(s) < 1$ can be uniformly approximated by vertical translates of $\zeta$. In the form now standard: for any compact set $K \subset \{s : 1/2 < \operatorname{Re}(s) < 1\}$ with connected complement, any continuous non-vanishing function $f$ on $K$ that is holomorphic in the interior, and any $\varepsilon > 0$,
\[
\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \operatorname{meas}\!\left\{\tau \in [0, T] : \max_{s \in K} |\zeta(s + i\tau) - f(s)| < \varepsilon \right\} > 0.
\]

Universality shows that the values of $\zeta(s)$ in the strip $1/2 < \sigma < 1$ imitate those of any admissible target function. It concerns approximation of non-vanishing functions and is therefore consistent with RH; Bagchi \cite{bagchi1987} showed that RH is equivalent to the statement that $\zeta$ can be approximated in this sense by its own vertical translates, that is, that the displayed inequality holds with $f=\zeta$ for every such $K$ and $\varepsilon$.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Why RH is hard: the role of the Euler product}\label{sec:whyhard}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

There exist Dirichlet series that satisfy a functional equation of the same type as that of $\zeta$ but have zeros off the critical line. The standard example is the Davenport--Heilbronn function, a linear combination of $L(s,\chi)$ and $L(s,\bar\chi)$ for a complex character $\chi$ modulo~5, with coefficients chosen so that the combination satisfies a functional equation. It has infinitely many zeros with $\operatorname{Re}(s)>1$ \cite[\S10.25]{titchmarsh1986}.

It follows that any proof of RH must use properties of $\zeta(s)$ beyond the functional equation. The most important one is the Euler product $\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}$. In particular:
\begin{itemize}
\item The geometric approaches (Section~\ref{sec:geometric}) must explain how the Euler product enters the intersection theory. For curves over $\mathbb{F}_q$ it enters through the Frobenius, whose fixed points are counted by the intersection numbers $\Delta\cdot\Gamma_n$; the analogous mechanism for $\mathbb{Z}$ has not been identified.
\item The spectral approaches (Section~\ref{sec:spectral}) must explain how the Euler product determines the operator. The Davenport--Heilbronn example treated in \cite{leclair2024} (Section~\ref{sec:bk}) illustrates this point.
\item The analytic approaches (Section~\ref{sec:analytic}) use the Euler product through the multiplicative structure of Dirichlet polynomials, but not in a way that is known to single out the critical line.
\end{itemize}

One may describe the difficulty as that of relating two descriptions of $\zeta(s)$: the multiplicative description by the Euler product, which involves the primes, and the description by the zeros, which statistically resemble the eigenvalues of a random matrix.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Quasicrystals and Dyson's proposal}\label{sec:quasi}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Dyson, in his essay ``Birds and Frogs'' \cite{dyson2009}, proposed a connection between the zeros and \emph{quasicrystals}, which he defines as distributions of discrete point masses whose Fourier transform is again a distribution of discrete point masses. If RH holds, the ordinates $\gamma$ of the zeros form a point set on the real line whose Fourier transform, by the explicit formula, consists of point masses at the points $\pm\log p^k$ ($p$ prime, $k \ge 1$) together with a locally integrable term coming from the archimedean factor. In this sense the zeros would form a one-dimensional quasicrystal.

Dyson suggested classifying one-dimensional quasicrystals and looking for the zeros in the classification. This is a proposal and not a technical programme: no classification of one-dimensional quasicrystals is known, and the proposal presupposes RH for the description of the zeros as a real point set.

We distinguish three levels among these conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Proved equivalent to RH.} Weil positivity ($W(g * \tilde{g}) \ge 0$ for all admissible $g$), Li's criterion ($\lambda_n \ge 0$ for all $n$), the condition $\Lambda\le0$, and the Nyman--Beurling--B\'aez-Duarte condition. The theorem of Bombieri and Lagarias (Section~\ref{sec:li}) relates the first two: the Li coefficients are values of the Weil functional.
\item \emph{Programmes with a precise target not known to imply RH, or not reached.} A Hodge index inequality on an arithmetic square, which cannot yet be stated; and global Weil positivity approached through the semilocal trace formula and the prolate operators, where the target is equivalent to RH but has not been reached.
\item \emph{Heuristic.} The unitarity condition $d(\Delta) \ge 0$ in conformal field theory, which has not been shown to constrain the zeros.
\end{enumerate}

It is possible that a proof of RH will establish one of the positivity conditions of level (i) or (ii); it is also possible that RH will be proved by other means and that these positivities will then follow as consequences. Nothing in the present state of knowledge decides between the two.

The evidence for RH is of three kinds: the verification of the first $1.2\times10^{13}$ zeros \cite{platt2021}; the theorems that most zeros are close to the line and that more than $5/12$ lie on it; and the agreement of zero statistics with random matrix predictions. None of these bears on a hypothetical zero off the line at very large height. The equivalent criteria of Section~\ref{sec:equiv} would each fail if such a zero existed, but being equivalent to RH they are not independent evidence.

\section{Directions for further work}\label{sec:paths}
%% ======================================================================

We list directions suggested by the developments surveyed above.

\subsection{The algebro-geometric direction}\label{sec:path-geom}

The aim is to complete Weil's strategy for $\mathbb{Z}$ by developing an intersection theory on the arithmetic square and proving a Hodge index theorem there. This would derive RH from the geometry of $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$. The Riemann--Roch formula of \cite{connes2024} is a step in the formalism; the obstacles of Section~\ref{sec:cc-critique} remain.

The steps needed are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Construct an intersection theory on the product $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}) \times_{\mathbb{S}} \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$, with intersection numbers for divisors.
\item Develop a Hodge theory, or a substitute, giving the positivity needed for a Hodge index theorem.
\item Identify the analogue of the Frobenius correspondences $\Gamma_n \subset C \times C$ for $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ and compute their intersection numbers.
\end{enumerate}

Each of these steps requires new ideas; none is a matter of technique only.

\subsection{The analytic direction}\label{sec:path-analytic}

The aim is to improve the large value estimates and density bounds, and the proportion of zeros on the critical line.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Improve the Guth--Maynard large value estimate, in the direction of the density hypothesis $N(\sigma, T) \ll T^{2(1-\sigma) + \varepsilon}$.
\item Increase the proven proportion of zeros on the critical line beyond $5/12$.
\item Extend the range of the short-interval prime number theorem below $17/30$; RH gives every $\theta>1/2$, and the asymptotic is conjectured for every $\theta>0$.
\end{enumerate}

Density estimates show that zeros off the line are rare, not that they are absent; proving $N(\sigma, T) = 0$ for $\sigma > 1/2$ is a different kind of statement.

\subsection{The spectral and bootstrap direction}\label{sec:path-spectral}

The aim is to exploit the relation between $\zeta(s)$ and conformal field theory given by the scalar modular bootstrap. If unitarity and modular invariance could be shown to be sufficiently restrictive, they might constrain the zeros.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Is there a unitary two-dimensional CFT with $U(1)^c$ symmetry whose scalar spectrum is determined by the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$?
\item Can numerical bootstrap methods (semidefinite programming) give rigorous bounds involving $\zeta(s)$ that are not accessible by analytic means?
\item Is there a relation between the crossing equation of Benjamin--Chang and Weil positivity?
\end{enumerate}

This direction is the most speculative of the three.

\subsection{Finite sections of Weil's criterion}\label{sec:weil-probe}

One can make Weil's criterion (Section~\ref{sec:connes-spectral}) concrete as follows. Fix a finite family $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_J$ of test functions localized near a height $T_0$ (for instance, in the variable $\log x$, Hermite functions of width $w$ modulated to the frequency $T_0$, the functional $W$ being extended to such functions) and form the Hermitian matrix
\[
Q_{jk}=W(\varphi_j*\tilde\varphi_k),
\]
computed from the arithmetic side of the explicit formula (prime powers, archimedean and polar terms). Since $\sum_{j,k}c_j\bar c_kQ_{jk}=W(g*\tilde g)$ with $g=\sum_jc_j\varphi_j$, Weil's criterion shows that RH implies $Q\succeq0$ for every such family, and that a zero off the line makes $Q$ fail to be positive semidefinite for a suitable family. A negative eigenvalue of $Q$ for one family would disprove RH, whereas positivity for finitely many families proves nothing: the content of RH is the positivity for all families simultaneously. Applied to a function with known zeros off the line, such as the Davenport--Heilbronn function (Section~\ref{sec:whyhard}), the construction gives a test of any proposed positivity argument: an argument that does not distinguish that function from $\zeta$ cannot be correct.

No computations of this kind are reported in this survey.

\subsection{Interaction between the approaches}\label{sec:interaction}

The directions described above are not mutually exclusive, and a proof of RH may combine elements of several. For example:
\begin{itemize}
\item Weil positivity, which appears in the work of Connes and in the geometric programme, is an inequality in harmonic analysis and might be approached by analytic methods.
\item The random matrix predictions, which originate in the spectral approach, have guided the development of analytic techniques (mollifiers, moment calculations).
\end{itemize}

The history of the Weil conjectures is instructive: Deligne's proof \cite{deligne1974} used methods (\'etale cohomology, the Lefschetz trace formula, Lefschetz pencils) that were not available when Weil formulated the conjectures. A proof of RH for $\mathbb{Q}$ may similarly require tools that do not yet exist.

%% ======================================================================
\section*{Concluding remarks}
%% ======================================================================

The Riemann Hypothesis has been open since 1859. The results surveyed here, in particular the Guth--Maynard theorem on zero density, the Riemann--Roch formula of Connes--Consani, and the reformulation in conformal field theory, do not bring a proof within reach.

\emph{Scope.} This survey has dealt with the Riemann Hypothesis for $\zeta(s)$. The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet $L$-functions and the Grand Riemann Hypothesis for automorphic $L$-functions are conjectures of the same nature, with consequences in algebraic number theory and arithmetic geometry; see Iwaniec--Kowalski \cite{iwaniec2004}. The spectral and geometric approaches discussed here are expected to apply to these generalizations as well.

\emph{Some open problems.} We list several problems adjacent to RH.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Large value estimates.} Extend the Guth--Maynard large value estimate to other ranges of $V$, or adapt the method to $L$-functions other than $\zeta(s)$. The database of \cite{tao2025} gives a systematic way to derive the consequences of such improvements.
\item \emph{Proportion of critical zeros.} Improve the proportion $5/12$.
\item \emph{Fyodorov--Hiary--Keating conjecture.} Determine the limiting distribution of the recentred maximum of $\log|\zeta(1/2+it)|$ on intervals of bounded length.
\item \emph{Intersection theory over $\mathbb{S}$.} Define an intersection pairing on $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}) \times_{\mathbb{S}} \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ and compute intersection numbers for natural divisors.
\item \emph{Bootstrap bounds.} Use the numerical conformal bootstrap to derive rigorous bounds from the Benjamin--Chang crossing equation.
\end{enumerate}
