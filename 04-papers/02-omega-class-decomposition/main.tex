\begin{abstract}
We split the Dirichlet polynomial
$D(t;N)=\sum_{2\le n\le N} n^{-1/2-it}$ into the sub-sums $S_k(t;N)$ over the
integers with $\omega(n)=k$ distinct prime factors.  Since there are
$K_N\le\log_2 N$ classes, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
$|D|^2\le K_N\sum_k|S_k|^2\le K_N^2\max_k|S_k|^2$.  Combined with the
classical approximation of $\zeta(\half+it)$ by its partial sum of length
$\lceil t\rceil$, this shows that a bound
$B(N)=\max_{t\in[N/10,N],\,k}|S_k(t;N)|^2\le N^{\alpha}$ implies
$\zeta(\half+it)\ll t^{\alpha/2}\log t$.  The implication uses only the
number of classes.  The hypothesis for every $\alpha>0$ is sufficient for
the Lindel\"of hypothesis; it is not known to follow from it, and already
for the class $k=1$ it is expected to be of the strength of the Riemann
hypothesis.  We also record the mean square of each $S_k$, which follows
from the mean value theorem of Montgomery and Vaughan.

The second part of the paper is numerical.  For
$N\in\{10^4,\,5\times10^4,\,10^5,\,1.5\times10^5\}$ we compute the class
sums on a grid of spacing $\pi/\log N$ in $t\in[N/10,N]$ and refine the
largest local maxima; the resulting values are lower bounds for $B(N)$.
On this range $\log B(N)/\log N$ stays between $0.41$ and $0.43$ and shows
no monotone trend; the class attaining the maximum changes from $k=2$ to
$k=3$ between $N=5\times10^4$ and $N=10^5$; the largest values of
$|S_k|^2$ at $N=1.5\times10^5$ exceed the mean square by factors between
$8.6$ and $131$; and at
the largest value found of $|S_3|^2$ the contributions of the five decades
of $n$ have nearly equal arguments (resultant length $0.95$).  The code
that produces every number, table and figure is supplied.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
The Lindel\"of hypothesis (LH) asserts that for every $\varepsilon > 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lindelof}
  \abs{\zeta(\half + it)} \ll_\varepsilon t^\varepsilon
  \qquad (t \to \infty).
\end{equation}
It follows from the Riemann hypothesis (RH) \cite[\S14.2]{titchmarsh1986}.
The converse implication is not known; LH is equivalent to the statement
that $N(\sigma,T+1)-N(\sigma,T)=o(\log T)$ for every $\sigma>\half$, where
$N(\sigma,T)$ counts the zeros $\beta+i\gamma$ of $\zeta$ with
$\beta>\sigma$, $0<\gamma\le T$ \cite[Theorem~13.5]{titchmarsh1986}.
The best published exponent known to us is that of Bourgain
\cite{bourgain2017}: $\abs{\zeta(\half+it)} \ll t^{13/84+\varepsilon}$.

The Dirichlet polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D_def}
  D(t;N) = \sum_{n=2}^{N} n^{-1/2 - it}
\end{equation}
satisfies $\zeta(\half+it)=1+D(t;N)+O(t^{-1/2})$ for $N=\lceil t\rceil$
(by \cite[Theorem~4.11]{titchmarsh1986}; see the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:master}).  We decompose $D$ according to the arithmetic
function $\omega(n)$, the number of distinct prime factors of~$n$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sk_def}
  S_k(t;N) = \sum_{\substack{2\le n \le N \\ \omega(n) = k}}
    n^{-1/2-it}, \qquad
  D(t;N) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_N} S_k(t;N),
\end{equation}
where $K_N = \max\{\omega(n) : 2 \le n \le N\}$.  The term $n=1$, the only
integer with $\omega(n)=0$, is kept apart.

We compare $|D|^2$ with the sum $E = \sum_k |S_k|^2$ through the ratio
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1pr_def}
  1 + r(t;N) = \frac{|D(t;N)|^2}{E(t;N)}\qquad (E(t;N)\neq0).
\end{equation}

The statements proved are the following.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Amplification bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:CS}):
    $|D|^2\le K_N E$, that is, $1+r \le K_N \le \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor$.
  \item Reduction to class sums (Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}):
    $|D|^2 \le K_N^2 \max_k|S_k|^2$.
  \item Number of classes (Theorem~\ref{thm:Kcount}):
    $K_N \sim \log N / \log\log N$; this is classical.
  \item A refinement (Theorem~\ref{thm:refined}):
    $1+r \le (\sum_k \sqrt{p_k})^2$ with $p_k=|S_k|^2/E$; the right-hand
    side is at most $K_N$, with equality only if all $p_k$ are equal.
  \item A conditional bound for $\zeta$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:master}):
    if $B(N) \le N^\alpha$ for all large $N$, where $B(N)$ is the maximum
    of $|S_k(t;N)|^2$ over $k$ and $t\in[N/10,N]$, then
    $\zeta(\half+it) \ll t^{\alpha/2}\log t$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Items (i)--(iv) are unconditional and elementary: (i), (ii) and (iv) are
the Cauchy--Schwarz and triangle inequalities, and they use no property of
$\omega$ beyond the number of classes.  Item~(v) is conditional on the
hypothesis on~$B(N)$ and uses the classical approximation of
$\zeta(\half+it)$ by a partial sum of its Dirichlet series.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{On the direction of the reduction.}
The chain Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} $\to$ Theorem~\ref{thm:master} gives
one implication: if $B(N)\le C_\varepsilon N^\varepsilon$ for every
$\varepsilon>0$, then LH holds (Corollary~\ref{cor:lindelof}).  The
converse is not known: $B(N)$ bounds each class sum separately, whereas LH
bounds only the total $1+\sum_kS_k$.  What is known unconditionally is the
mean square of each $S_k$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:stronger}).  Moreover the
hypothesis is expected to be much stronger than LH: already for $k=1$, where
$S_1$ is a sum over prime powers, a bound $|S_1(t;N)|\ll N^{\varepsilon}$
for $t\asymp N$ is of the type that is known only under RH
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).  The paper therefore does not make progress
on LH; it records the elementary inequalities, and measures the class sums
numerically.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Numerical results.}
We compute lower bounds for $B(N)$ at four values of~$N$ up to
$1.5 \times 10^5$ (Section~\ref{sec:computation}).  We find:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The exponent $\alpha_B(N)=\log B(N)/\log N$ lies between $0.41$
    and $0.43$ on this range, with no monotone trend.
  \item The class attaining the maximum changes from $k = 2$ to $k = 3$
    between $N=5\times10^4$ and $N = 10^5$.
  \item The mean square of $S_k$ agrees with the value given by the mean
    value theorem, while the largest values of $|S_k|^2$ found exceed the
    mean by factors between $8.6$ and $131$ at $N=1.5\times10^5$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Setup and Notation}\label{sec:setup}

Throughout, $N \ge 2$ is an integer and $t \in \R$.
We write $\omega(n)$ for the number of distinct prime factors of~$n$
(with $\omega(1) = 0$).  The notations $f \ll g$ and $f = O(g)$ are
synonymous; $f \ll_\varepsilon g$ means that the implied constant may
depend on~$\varepsilon$.  Logarithms are natural unless a base is shown.

\begin{definition}[Dirichlet polynomial and $\omega$-class sums]
\label{def:D_and_Sk}
Let $a=(a_n)_{n\ge2}$ be complex numbers and let
$K_N=\max\{\omega(n):2\le n\le N\}$.  Define
\begin{align}
  D_a(t;N) &= \sum_{n=2}^{N} a_n \, n^{-1/2-it}, \label{eq:DF}\\
  S_{a,k}(t;N) &= \sum_{\substack{2\le n \le N \\ \omega(n) = k}}
    a_n \, n^{-1/2-it}, \qquad k = 1, 2, \ldots, K_N. \label{eq:SkF}
\end{align}
For $a_n = 1$ for all~$n$ we write $D=D_\zeta$ and $S_k$; these are the
sums \eqref{eq:D_def} and \eqref{eq:Sk_def}.  When the sequence $a$ is
clear we also write $S_k$ for $S_{a,k}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Energy, cross terms, amplification ratio]
\label{def:energy}
\begin{align}
  E(t;N) &= \sum_{k=1}^{K_N} |S_k(t;N)|^2, \label{eq:E}\\
  X(t;N) &= |D(t;N)|^2 - E(t;N)
    = 2 \sum_{j < k} \re\bigl(\overline{S_j} \, S_k\bigr), \label{eq:X}\\
  r(t;N) &= \frac{X(t;N)}{E(t;N)}, \qquad
  1 + r(t;N) = \frac{|D(t;N)|^2}{E(t;N)}\qquad(E(t;N)\ne0). \label{eq:r}
\end{align}
The same definitions apply to a general sequence $a$, with $D_a$,
$S_{a,k}$, $E_a$, $r_a$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Maximum class sum and its exponent]
\label{def:BN}
For the sequence $a_n=1$,
\begin{align}
  B(N) &= \max_{\substack{t \in [N/10, N] \\
    1 \le k \le K_N}} |S_k(t;N)|^2, \label{eq:BN}\\
  \alpha_B(N) &= \frac{\log B(N)}{\log N}. \label{eq:alphaB}
\end{align}
We write $k^*(N)$ for a class attaining the maximum in \eqref{eq:BN}.
\end{definition}

When $E(t)\ne0$ the energy fractions are $p_k(t) = |S_k(t)|^2 / E(t)$;
they satisfy $\sum_k p_k = 1$.

\section{The amplification bound}\label{sec:thm1}

\begin{theorem}[Amplification bound]\label{thm:CS}
For any complex sequence $a$, any $N \ge 2$ and any $t \in \R$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CS_bound}
  |D_a(t;N)|^2 \le K_N\,E_a(t;N),
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if $S_{a,1}=S_{a,2}=\dots=S_{a,K_N}$.  In
particular $1 + r_a(t;N) \le K_N$ whenever $E_a(t;N)\ne0$.
Moreover, $K_N \le \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality applied to the $K_N$ complex numbers
$S_1, \ldots, S_{K_N}$ and the vector $(1,\dots,1)$,
\[
  \abs{\sum_{k=1}^{K_N} S_k}^2
  \le K_N \sum_{k=1}^{K_N} |S_k|^2,
\]
with equality if and only if $(S_1,\dots,S_{K_N})$ is a multiple of
$(1,\dots,1)$, that is, if and only if all $S_k$ are equal.  This is
\eqref{eq:CS_bound}; dividing by $E\neq0$ gives $1+r\le K_N$.

For the bound on $K_N$: every integer $n \ge 2$ satisfies
$n \ge \prod_{p \mid n} p \ge 2^{\omega(n)}$.  Therefore
$\omega(n) \le \log_2 n \le \log_2 N$ for $n\le N$, and
$K_N \le \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Equality $1+r = K_N$ requires all $K_N$ class sums to be equal as complex
numbers.  In the computations of Section~\ref{sec:computation} the largest
value found of $1+r$ lies between $4.1$ and $4.9$, about $17$--$24\%$
below $K_N$ (Table~\ref{tab:BN}).
\end{remark}

\section{Reduction to class sums}\label{sec:thm2}

\begin{theorem}[Reduction]\label{thm:reduction}
For any complex sequence $a$, any $N \ge 2$ and $t \in \R$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduction}
  |D_a(t;N)|^2 \le K_N^2 \cdot
  \max_{1 \le k \le K_N} |S_{a,k}(t;N)|^2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:CS}, $|D|^2 \le K_N \cdot E$, and
$E = \sum_{k=1}^{K_N} |S_k|^2 \le K_N \cdot \max_k |S_k|^2$.
(Equivalently, $|D|\le\sum_k|S_k|\le K_N\max_k|S_k|$.)
\end{proof}

The argument uses only the number of classes: the same inequality holds
for any partition of $\{2,\dots,N\}$ into $K$ classes, with $K$ in place
of $K_N$, and it loses only a factor $N^{o(1)}$ whenever $K=N^{o(1)}$.

\begin{corollary}[A sufficient condition for the Lindel\"of hypothesis]
\label{cor:lindelof}
If for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $C_\varepsilon$ such that
$B(N) \le C_\varepsilon N^\varepsilon$ for all $N\ge2$, then the Lindel\"of
hypothesis holds.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $\varepsilon>0$.  Then $B(N)\le N^{2\varepsilon}$ for all sufficiently
large $N$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:master} below (whose proof does not use the
present corollary) with $\alpha = 2\varepsilon$ gives
$\zeta(\half+it)\ll t^{\varepsilon}\log t$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Mean square of the class sums]
\label{prop:stronger}
Let $N\ge3$, $1\le k\le K_N$ and $T>0$.  Then
\[
  \frac1T\int_0^T |S_k(t;N)|^2\,dt
  =\sum_{\substack{2\le n\le N\\ \omega(n)=k}}\frac1n
  +O\Bigl(\frac{\pi_k(N)}{T}\Bigr),
  \qquad \pi_k(N)=\#\{n\le N:\omega(n)=k\},
\]
with an absolute implied constant, and
\[
  \sum_{\substack{2\le n\le N\\ \omega(n)=k}}\frac1n
  \le\frac{(\log\log N+c_0)^k}{k!}
\]
for an absolute constant $c_0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the mean value theorem of Montgomery and Vaughan
\cite[Corollary~3]{montgomery-vaughan1974},
\[
  \int_0^T\Bigl|\sum_{n\le N}b_nn^{-it}\Bigr|^2dt
  =\sum_{n\le N}|b_n|^2\bigl(T+O(n)\bigr)
\]
for any complex $b_n$.  Taking $b_n=n^{-1/2}$ if $\omega(n)=k$ and $b_n=0$
otherwise gives the first statement.  For the second, every $n$ with
$\omega(n)=k$ can be written in exactly $k!$ ways as an ordered product
$q_1\cdots q_k$ of powers of pairwise distinct primes, and each
$q_i\le N$.  Hence
\[
  k!\sum_{\substack{n\le N\\ \omega(n)=k}}\frac1n
  \le\Bigl(\sum_{p^a\le N}\frac1{p^a}\Bigr)^k,
\]
and
$\sum_{p^a\le N}p^{-a}\le\sum_{p\le N}1/p+\sum_p1/(p(p-1))
=\log\log N+O(1)$ by Mertens' theorem.
\end{proof}

Thus the mean square of each class sum over $0\le t\le T$, $T\ge N$, is at
most $(\log\log N+c_0)^k/k!+O(1)$, which is far below $N^\varepsilon$.
The hypothesis of Corollary~\ref{cor:lindelof} concerns instead the
maximum over $t\in[N/10,N]$.  Corollary~\ref{cor:lindelof} is an
implication only: we do not know whether LH implies its hypothesis, since
LH bounds the total $1+\sum_kS_k$ and not the individual~$S_k$.  The ratio
between the largest values found and the mean square is tabulated in
Section~\ref{sec:computation} (Table~\ref{tab:Rk}).

\section{The number of classes}\label{sec:thm3}

\begin{theorem}[Class count]\label{thm:Kcount}
$K_N = \max\{\omega(n) : n \le N\}$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $K_N \le \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor$.
  \item $K_N \sim \dfrac{\log N}{\log\log N}$ as $N \to \infty$.
  \item $K_N=\max\{m: P_m\le N\}$, where $P_m = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_m$ is
    the product of the first $m$ primes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Parts (b) and (c) are classical; see \cite[\S22.10]{hardy-wright2008}.
We include the short proof.

\begin{proof}
Part~(a) is proved in Theorem~\ref{thm:CS}.

For~(c): among all $n$ with $\omega(n) = m$, the smallest is
$P_m = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots p_m$, since such an $n$ is divisible by
$m$ distinct primes and their product is at least $P_m$.  Therefore
$K_N = \max\{m : P_m \le N\}$.

For~(b): by the prime number theorem,
$\log P_m = \sum_{j\le m} \log p_j=\theta(p_m)\sim p_m \sim m \log m$.
Since $P_{K_N}\le N<P_{K_N+1}$, we get $\log N\sim K_N\log K_N$, whence
$\log\log N\sim\log K_N$ and $K_N\sim\log N/\log\log N$.
\end{proof}

For orientation, some numerical values:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
$N$ & $\lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor$ & $K_N$ &
$\log N / \log\log N$ \\
\midrule
$10^4$ & 13 & 5 & 4.15 \\
$10^5$ & 16 & 6 & 4.71 \\
$10^6$ & 19 & 7 & 5.26 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For instance $P_5=2310 \le 10^4<P_6=30030$, so $K_{10^4} = 5$.

\section{A refinement in terms of the energy fractions}
\label{sec:thm4}

\begin{theorem}[Refined amplification bound]\label{thm:refined}
For any $t$ and $N$ with $E(t;N)\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:refined}
  1 + r(t;N) \le \Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{K_N} \sqrt{p_k(t)}\Bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
where $p_k(t) = |S_k(t)|^2 / E(t)$ are the energy fractions.

If a class $k^*$ carries energy fraction $p_{k^*}(t) \ge \delta$ for some
$\delta\ge 1/K_N$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:refined_delta}
  1 + r(t;N) \le \bigl(\sqrt{\delta} +
    \sqrt{(K_N-1)(1-\delta)}\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The condition $\delta\ge1/K_N$ cannot be dropped in the inequality for general class
sums: if all $S_{a,k}$ are equal and nonzero then $1+r_a=K_N$, while the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:refined_delta} with $\delta=0$ is $K_N-1$.

\begin{proof}
By the triangle inequality,
$|D| = |\sum_k S_k| \le \sum_k |S_k|$.
Writing $|S_k| = \sqrt{p_k E}$,
\[
  |D|^2 \le \Bigl(\sum_k \sqrt{p_k E}\Bigr)^2
  = E \cdot \Bigl(\sum_k \sqrt{p_k}\Bigr)^2.
\]
Dividing by $E$ gives~\eqref{eq:refined}.

For~\eqref{eq:refined_delta}: since
$\sum_{k \ne k^*} p_k = 1 - p_{k^*}$, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
\[
  \sum_{k \ne k^*} \sqrt{p_k}
  \le \sqrt{(K_N-1)\sum_{k \ne k^*} p_k}
  = \sqrt{(K_N-1)(1-p_{k^*})},
\]
so that $\sum_k\sqrt{p_k}\le f(p_{k^*})$ with
$f(p)=\sqrt p+\sqrt{(K_N-1)(1-p)}$.  For $0<p<1$,
\[
  f'(p)=\frac1{2\sqrt p}-\frac{\sqrt{K_N-1}}{2\sqrt{1-p}}\le0
  \iff 1-p\le (K_N-1)p\iff p\ge\frac1{K_N},
\]
so $f$ is non-increasing on $[1/K_N,1]$.  As
$p_{k^*}\ge\delta\ge1/K_N$, we get $f(p_{k^*})\le f(\delta)$, which is
\eqref{eq:refined_delta}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:refinement_numerical}
For illustration: if $p_{k^*}(t)=0.42$ and $K_N = 6$, the bound
\eqref{eq:refined_delta} is
$(\sqrt{0.42} + \sqrt{5 \cdot 0.58})^2 = (0.648 + 1.703)^2\approx 5.53$,
which is $8\%$ below $K_N$; if $p_{k^*}(t)=0.42$ and $K_N = 5$ it is
$4.71$, which is $6\%$ below $K_N$.
\end{remark}

\section{A conditional bound for \texorpdfstring{$\zeta$}{zeta}}\label{sec:thm5}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional bound]\label{thm:master}
Let $\alpha\ge0$ and suppose that $B(N) \le N^\alpha$ for all sufficiently
large $N$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:master}
  \zeta(\half + it) \ll t^{\alpha/2}\log t\qquad(t\to\infty).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: approximation of $\zeta$ by a partial sum.}
By \cite[Theorem~4.11]{titchmarsh1986}, for a fixed constant $C>1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AFE}
  \zeta(s) = \sum_{n \le x} n^{-s}-\frac{x^{1-s}}{1-s} + O(x^{-\sigma})
\end{equation}
uniformly for $s=\sigma+it$ with $\sigma\ge\sigma_0>0$ and
$|t|<2\pi x/C$.  Let $t\ge2$, $s=\half+it$ and $x=N=\lceil t\rceil$, so
that $|t|\le x<2\pi x/C$ with $C=2$.  Since $\sum_{n\le N}n^{-s}=1+D_\zeta(t;N)$ and
$|N^{1/2-it}/(\half-it)|\le N^{1/2}/t\le 2t^{-1/2}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AFE_simple}
  \zeta(\half+it) = 1+D_\zeta(t;N) + O(t^{-1/2}),\qquad N=\lceil t\rceil.
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: the range of $t$.}
In Definition~\ref{def:BN} the maximum is over $t \in [N/10, N]$.  For
$N=\lceil t\rceil\ge2$ we have $N/10\le N-1<t\le N$, so the pair $(t,N)$
is admissible and $|S_k(t;N)|^2\le B(N)$ for every $k$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}.}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}, the bound $K_N\le\log_2N$ and the
hypothesis, for $t$ large and $N=\lceil t\rceil\le t+1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:master_chain}
  |D_\zeta(t;N)|^2 \le K_N^2 \, B(N) \le (\log_2 N)^2 N^\alpha
  \le \bigl(\log_2 (t+1)\bigr)^2 (t+1)^{\alpha}.
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 4: conclusion.}
From~\eqref{eq:AFE_simple} and~\eqref{eq:master_chain},
\[
  |\zeta(\half+it)|\le 1+\log_2(t+1)\,(t+1)^{\alpha/2}+O(t^{-1/2})
  \ll t^{\alpha/2}\log t .\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

The size of $\alpha$ is to be read as follows.  Trivially
$|S_k(t;N)|\le\sum_{n\le N}n^{-1/2}\le2\sqrt N$, so $B(N)\le 4N$, which
corresponds to $\alpha=1$ and gives only $\zeta(\half+it)\ll t^{1/2}\log t$.
The convexity bound $t^{1/4}$ is matched for $\alpha=\half$, the exponent
$13/84$ of \cite{bourgain2017} for $\alpha=13/42\approx0.3095$, and LH
corresponds to the hypothesis for every $\alpha>0$.  The hypothesis
cannot hold with $\alpha<0$: by the mean value theorem
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) the mean of $|S_1(t;N)|^2$ over
$t\in[N/10,N]$ is $\sum_{p^a\le N}p^{-a}+O(1/\log N)$, which tends to
infinity with~$N$.

\begin{remark}[Explicit form]\label{rem:effective}
Before the logarithm is absorbed, the proof gives, for $t\ge2$ and
$N=\lceil t\rceil$,
\[
  |\zeta(\half+it)|
  \;\le\; 1+K_N\,B(N)^{1/2}+O(t^{-1/2}),
\]
with an absolute implied constant; here the $1$ is the term $n=1$, the
factor $K_N\le\log_2(t+1)$ is the loss in Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}, and
the error term is that of~\eqref{eq:AFE_simple}.  At a given $t$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:refined} allows one factor $K_N^{1/2}$ to be replaced by
$\sum_k\sqrt{p_k(t)}$, since
$|D|\le E^{1/2}\sum_k\sqrt{p_k}\le (K_NB(N))^{1/2}\sum_k\sqrt{p_k}$; this
does not change the exponent.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Square-root scaling]\label{prop:sqrt_scaling}
For an integer $M\ge2$ let
\[
  \widetilde B(M)=\max\bigl\{|S_k(t;M)|^2:\ 1\le k\le K_M,\
  2\pi M^2\le t\le2\pi(M+1)^2\bigr\}.
\]
Let $\alpha\ge0$. If $\widetilde B(M)\le M^\alpha$ for all large~$M$, then
$\zeta(\half+it)\ll t^{\alpha/4}\log t$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The approximate functional equation
\cite[Theorem~4.13]{titchmarsh1986} states that, for $0<\sigma<1$, $x,y\ge h>0$ and $2\pi xy=t$,
\[
  \zeta(s)=\sum_{n\le x}n^{-s}+\chi(s)\sum_{n\le y}n^{s-1}
  +O(x^{-\sigma})+O(t^{1/2-\sigma}y^{\sigma-1}).
\]
Take $s=\half+it$ and $x=y=\sqrt{t/2\pi}$, and let
$M=\lfloor\sqrt{t/2\pi}\rfloor$, so that
$2\pi M^2\le t<2\pi(M+1)^2$.  Both sums have absolute value
$|1+D_\zeta(t;M)|$ and $|\chi(\half+it)|=1$, hence
$|\zeta(\half+it)|\le 2\,|1+D_\zeta(t;M)| + O(t^{-1/4})$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction},
$|D_\zeta(t;M)|\le K_M\,\widetilde B(M)^{1/2}\le(\log_2 M)\,M^{\alpha/2}
\ll t^{\alpha/4}\log t$.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
In Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrt_scaling} the trivial bound
$|S_k(t;M)|^2\le4M$ corresponds to $\alpha=1$ and recovers the convexity
bound $t^{1/4}$ up to the logarithm; improving on convexity requires
$\alpha<1$, and reaching the exponent $13/84$ requires $\alpha\le13/21$.
The price is that $|S_k(t;M)|$ must be controlled for $t\asymp M^2$
instead of $t\asymp M$.  The computations of
Section~\ref{sec:computation} are made in the scaling $t\asymp N$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:master}.
\end{remark}

\section{Computational Results}\label{sec:computation}

We compute the class sums for
$N \in \{10^4, 5 \times 10^4, 10^5, 1.5 \times 10^5\}$ on the grid
\[
  t_j=\frac N{10}+j\,\Delta t,\qquad \Delta t=\frac{\pi}{\log N},\qquad
  t_j\le N ,
\]
without thinning.  As a function of $t$, $|S_k(t;N)|^2$ is a trigonometric
polynomial with frequencies $\log(m/n)$, $|\log(m/n)|\le\log N$, so
$\Delta t$ is its critical sampling interval; the grid determines the mean
square but does not locate the maxima.  For each $k$ the $40$ largest
local maxima of $|S_k(t_j;N)|^2$ on the grid, and likewise those of
$1+r$, are therefore refined on $[t_j-\Delta t,t_j+\Delta t]$ by three
successive $17$-point subdivisions.  All maxima reported below are the
largest values found in this way.  They are \emph{lower bounds} for the
true maxima over $[N/10,N]$, and the quantities derived from them
(the exponent $\alpha_B$, the ratios in Table~\ref{tab:Rk}) are lower
bounds as well; we keep the notation $B(N)$, $\alpha_B(N)$ for these
computed values.  The code is described in Appendix~\ref{app:code}.

\subsection{The growth of \texorpdfstring{$B(N)$}{B(N)}}

Four values of $N$ within little more than one decade, each a lower
bound, do not determine the behaviour of $\alpha_B(N)$ as $N\to\infty$.
A second normalisation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alphaB_model}
  c_B(N) = \frac{\log B(N)}{\log\log N},
  \qquad\text{so that } B(N)=(\log N)^{c_B(N)} .
\end{equation}
The computed values are $c_B=1.78$, $1.86$, $1.96$, $2.02$ for the four
values of $N$, so that $B(N)$ lies between $(\log N)^{1.7}$ and
$(\log N)^{2.1}$ on this range, while the slope of $\log B$ against
$\log\log N$ between the first and the last value of $N$ is about~$4.1$.
On this range the data do not determine the behaviour of $\alpha_B$: they
are compatible with $B(N)$ growing like a power of $\log N$, which would
give $\alpha_B\to0$, and equally with $\alpha_B$ bounded below.  Since the
cost of the computation grows like $N^2\log N$, a substantially larger
range would require a different algorithm.

of the computed values and not an explanation of them.

while $1+r(t^*;N)=0.022$, that is, $|D(t^*;N)|^2=7.2$.  So the largest

almost completely and the full polynomial $D$ is of ordinary size.  This
is a concrete instance of the remark following
Proposition~\ref{prop:stronger}: a bound for $D$, and hence for $\zeta$,
does not by itself bound the class sums.  It is qualitatively what the
formal relation $S_k\approx S_1^k/k!$ (which ignores the truncation at $N$
and the terms with repeated primes) suggests: a large value of $|S_1|$
makes several $|S_k|$ large at the same time, whereas
$\sum_{k\ge1}S_1^k/k!=e^{S_1}-1$ has modulus governed by $\re S_1$ alone.
We do not make this precise.

simultaneously as $N\to\infty$, or bound the class sums directly, for
instance as multilinear sums (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{The Liouville function: completely multiplicative coefficients
and large values}\label{sec:liouville}

One may ask whether the large values of the class sums are tied to the
coefficients $a_n=1$.  They are not tied to the absence of sign changes,
nor are they excluded by complete multiplicativity of the coefficients.
The Liouville function $\lambda(n)=(-1)^{\Omega(n)}$, where $\Omega(n)$ is
the number of prime factors of $n$ counted with multiplicity, is
completely multiplicative, and
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\lambda(n)}{n^s}=\frac{\zeta(2s)}{\zeta(s)}
=\prod_p(1+p^{-s})^{-1}
\qquad(\re s>1).
\]
The polynomial $D_\lambda(t;N)=\sum_{n\le N}\lambda(n)n^{-1/2-it}$ (here
including $n=1$) is a partial sum of this Dirichlet series on the line
$\re s=\half$, and the function $\zeta(2s)/\zeta(s)$ has poles at the
zeros of $\zeta$ on that line.

\begin{observation}[A large value of $D_\lambda$ near zeros of $\zeta$]
\label{obs:liouville}
For $N=10^5$ and $t=7563.5$ one finds $|D_\lambda(t;N)|=46.88$, whereas
$(\sum_{n\le N}1/n)^{1/2}=3.48$ is the root mean square of
$D_\lambda(\,\cdot\,;N)$ over long ranges of $t$ given by the mean value
theorem.  The point $t$ lies among the consecutive zeros of $\zeta$ with
ordinates $7563.18$, $7563.52$, $7563.77$, and
$|\zeta(\half+it)|=0.0128$,
$|\zeta(1+2it)/\zeta(\half+it)|=136.7$.  If $D_\lambda$ is split according
to $\omega(n)$ (the term $n=1$ forming the class $k=0$), then
$|D_\lambda|=0.93\sum_k|S_{\lambda,k}|$ at this point.
\end{observation}

So complete multiplicativity of the coefficients does not prevent large
values of the partial sums, and the alignment of the class sums is not
special to $a_n=1$.  In this example the large value occurs next to a
cluster of zeros of $\zeta$, as the factor $1/\zeta(s)$ suggests.

\subsection{The ratio \texorpdfstring{$1+r$}{1+r} at large values of \texorpdfstring{$|D|$}{|D|}}\label{sec:peaks}

The ratio $1+r=|D|^2/E$ can also be examined at the points where $|D|$ is
largest.  These are the large values of $\zeta$ in the range considered:
by \eqref{eq:AFE} with $x=N$, as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:master}, $\zeta(\half+it)=1+D(t;N)+O(N^{-1/2})$
uniformly for $t\in[N/10,N]$.  For each $N$ we take the
$1\%$ of the grid points $t_j\in[N/10,N]$ with the largest $|D(t_j;N)|^2$
and compare the mean of $1+r$ over these points with its mean over the
whole grid.

\begin{observation}[$1+r$ at large values of $|D|$]
\label{obs:peaks}
For $N=10^4$, $5\times10^4$, $10^5$, $1.5\times10^5$ the mean of $1+r$
over the $1\%$ of grid points with the largest $|D|^2$ is $2.89$, $2.99$,
$3.00$, $3.04$, against a mean over the whole grid of $0.68$, $0.69$,
$0.69$, $0.69$.  The smallest value of $1+r$ among these points is $1.07$,
$0.91$, $0.77$, $0.67$.  Thus at the largest values of $|D|$ on the grid
the class sums add constructively on average, with $1+r$ about one half
of $K_N$, and to the same extent at the four values of~$N$.
\end{observation}

That $1+r$ is larger where $|D|$ is large is to be expected, since
$|D|^2=(1+r)E$; the content of the observation is the size of the effect
and the absence of any visible change of behaviour between the values of
$N$ at which $k^*=2$ and those at which $k^*=3$.

\section{Discussion and Open Problems}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Connection to bilinear and multilinear estimates}

The class sum $S_2$ is
\[
  S_2(t;N) = \sum_{p<q}\ \sum_{\substack{a,b\ge1\\ p^aq^b\le N}}
  (p^aq^b)^{-1/2-it},
\]
whose main part $\sum_{p<q,\ pq\le N}(pq)^{-1/2-it}$ is a bilinear form in
the primes; similarly the main part of $S_3$ is a trilinear form.  Sums of
this shape occur in the treatment of sums over primes by the identities of
Vaughan and Heath-Brown \cite{heath-brown1982},
\cite[Chapter~13]{iwaniec-kowalski2004}, where they are estimated by
bilinear methods.  We do not know of estimates of this kind that apply in
the range $t\asymp N$ with the strength required here.

\subsection{Mean values of the class sums}
\label{subsec:moments}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:stronger},
\[
  \frac1T\int_0^T|S_k(t;N)|^2\,dt
  =\sum_{\substack{n\le N\\ \omega(n)=k}}\frac1n+O\Bigl(\frac NT\Bigr).
\]
For fixed $k$ the sum on the right is asymptotic to
$(\log\log N)^k/k!$ as $N\to\infty$: the upper bound is in
Proposition~\ref{prop:stronger}, and the squarefree $n=p_1\cdots p_k$ with
all $p_i\le N^{1/k}$ contribute at least
$\frac1{k!}\bigl(u^k-\binom k2u^{k-2}\sum_pp^{-2}\bigr)$ with
$u=\sum_{p\le N^{1/k}}1/p=\log\log N+O_k(1)$.  Consequently the classes
that carry most of the mean square are those with $k$ of the order of
$\log\log N$ (for $N=1.5\times10^5$, $\log\log N=2.48$ and the largest
mean is that of $k=2$; see Table~\ref{tab:Rk}), and the share of any fixed
class in $\sum_k\int_0^T|S_k|^2$ tends to zero as $N\to\infty$.  In
particular, for fixed $k$ and every $\delta>0$,
$\int_0^T|S_k(t;N)|^2dt\ll_{k,\delta}T(\log N)^{\delta}$ for $T\ge N$.

For the fourth power there is the pointwise identity
\[
  |D|^4=(E+X)^2=\sum_k|S_k|^4+\sum_{j\ne k}|S_j|^2|S_k|^2+\bigl(2EX+X^2\bigr),
\]
which separates the terms involving one class, the products of the
squared moduli of two classes, and the remainder, which involves the
arguments of the class sums.  This identity concerns the Dirichlet
polynomial $D$; we do not derive from it any statement on the moments of
$\zeta$.
On the grids of Section~\ref{sec:computation} the three groups account for
the following shares of the grid mean of $|D|^4$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rccc}
\toprule
$N$ & $\sum_k|S_k|^4$ & $\sum_{j\ne k}|S_j|^2|S_k|^2$ & $2EX+X^2$ \\
\midrule
$10\,000$  & $18.7\%$ & $30.2\%$ & $51.1\%$ \\
$50\,000$  & $14.8\%$ & $26.8\%$ & $58.4\%$ \\
$100\,000$ & $13.6\%$ & $25.6\%$ & $60.7\%$ \\
$150\,000$ & $13.1\%$ & $25.2\%$ & $61.8\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
More than half of the mean fourth power of $|D|$ thus comes from the
remainder, and its share increases slowly on this range.  This is a
numerical observation at four values of~$N$.

\subsection{The mean value theorem and its limitations}

By \cite[Corollary~3]{montgomery-vaughan1974},
\[
  \int_{N/10}^{N} |S_k(t;N)|^2\,dt
  = \frac{9N}{10} \sum_{\substack{n \le N \\ \omega(n) = k}} \frac1n
  +O\bigl(\pi_k(N)\bigr),
\]
so that the mean of $|S_k|^2$ over $[N/10,N]$ equals
$\sum_{\omega(n)=k,\,n\le N}1/n$ up to an error $O(\pi_k(N)/N)$.  The
third and fourth columns of Table~\ref{tab:Rk} show this agreement.
The gap between the mean and the largest values is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:resonance}.

Obtaining pointwise bounds on $|S_k|^2$ is the problem.  The methods of
van der Corput and Vinogradov, which give pointwise bounds for
$\sum_{n\le N}n^{-it}$, use the regularity of the phase $t\log n$ along
all integers of an interval and do not apply directly to sums restricted
to $\omega(n)=k$.

\subsection{Open problems}

\begin{conjecture}[$\alpha_B$ decay]\label{conj:decay}
$\alpha_B(N) \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$; here $B(N)$ is the true maximum
of Definition~\ref{def:BN}.
\end{conjecture}

Conjecture~\ref{conj:decay} is the hypothesis of
Corollary~\ref{cor:lindelof}, so it implies the Lindel\"of hypothesis.
The computations of Section~\ref{sec:computation} neither support nor
contradict it.

\begin{conjecture}[Uniform class bound]\label{conj:uniform}
For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $C_\varepsilon$ such that for all
$N\ge2$ and every $k \le K_N$,
\[
  \max_{t \in [N/10, N]} |S_k(t;N)|^2
  \le C_{\varepsilon} \, N^\varepsilon.
\]
\end{conjecture}

Conjecture~\ref{conj:uniform} is a restatement of
Conjecture~\ref{conj:decay}.  Already the case $k=1$, in which $S_1$ is a
sum over prime powers, is far beyond present knowledge.  For $t\asymp N$
the known bounds for $\sum_{p\le N}p^{-it}$, which rest on the zero-free
region of Vinogradov and Korobov (see \cite[Chapter~VI]{titchmarsh1986}
and the notes to that chapter), save less than any power of $N$ over the
trivial bound, whereas the conjecture asks for square-root cancellation.
Heuristically, by the explicit formula, a zero of $\zeta$ with real part
$\beta>\half$ and ordinate $\gamma\in[N/10,N]$ contributes to
$S_1(\gamma;N)$ a term of size about $N^{\beta-1/2}/\log N$.  For this
reason the hypothesis $B(N)\le C_\varepsilon N^\varepsilon$ is expected to
be of the strength of RH, and not merely of LH; we have no proof of
either implication between it and RH.

\begin{question}[Sequence of maximising classes]\label{conj:transitions}
Is $k^*(N)$ eventually non-decreasing in $N$?  If so, how do its jump
points $N_3 < N_4 < \cdots$ grow, where $N_j$ is the least $N$ from which
on $k^*(N)\ge j$?
\end{question}

In our computations the class attaining the maximum is $k^*=2$ for
$N\le5\times10^4$ and $k^*=3$ for $N=10^5$ and $1.5\times10^5$.  One
change of class does not indicate how the sequence continues.

\appendix
\section{Reproduction Code}\label{app:code}

The numbers in Tables~\ref{tab:BN}--\ref{tab:resonance}, in
Observations~\ref{obs:liouville} and~\ref{obs:peaks} and in
Section~\ref{subsec:moments}, and
Figures~\ref{fig:alphaB}--\ref{fig:resonance}, are produced by
the script \texttt{reproduce.py} supplied with the paper; it needs no
external data and writes the file
\texttt{results/computation\_results.json}, from which the tables were
transcribed, and the four figures.  Its steps are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sieve.} Compute $\omega(n)$ for $n \le N$ with a sieve
    over the primes.
  \item \textbf{Grid.} Take $t_j=N/10+j\,\Delta t$,
    $\Delta t = \pi / \log N$, $t_j\le N$; this is $26\,386$, $154\,982$, $329\,822$ and $512\,156$ points for
    the four values of $N$.
  \item \textbf{Class sums.} For each~$t_j$ compute
    $S_k(t_j;N) = \sum_{\omega(n)=k} n^{-1/2-it_j}$, $k = 1, \ldots, K_N$,
    in double precision, and from them $|D|^2$, $E$ and $1+r$.
  \item \textbf{Refinement.} For each $k$, and for $1+r$, refine the $40$
    largest local maxima on the grid: on $[t_j-\Delta t,t_j+\Delta t]$
    evaluate at $17$ equally spaced points, re-centre at the largest
    value with half-width equal to the spacing, and repeat three times
    (final resolution $\Delta t/512$).
  \item \textbf{Output.} $B(N)$, $k^*$, $\alpha_B$, the per-class means
    and maxima, the size-bin decomposition at the largest values of
    $|S_2|^2$ and $|S_3|^2$, the statistics of $1+r$, and the Liouville
    polynomial of Observation~\ref{obs:liouville}.
\end{enumerate}

The computation of the class sums for the four values of $N$ took about
one hour with $16$ processes.  With the option \texttt{--validate}
the script compares $D_\zeta(t;10^4)$ at three random $t\in[10^3,10^4]$
with a $50$-digit evaluation; the absolute differences were below
$10^{-11}$.  With the same option it computes, in $20$-digit arithmetic,
the zeros of $\zeta$ and the values of $\zeta$ quoted in
Observation~\ref{obs:liouville}.  The file \texttt{reproduce\_colab.py} is a shorter
single-file version that computes the grid values (without refinement)
for one value of $N$; its output agrees with the grid maxima given in
parentheses in Table~\ref{tab:BN}.
